# 117110-117140 — The Opening of the Unification War

## 117,111 - The Provincial Integration Offer

In early **117,111**, the Roman Senate passes the **Lex Integrationis Necessariae**, usually translated as the Necessary Integration Act.

It authorises the Republic to offer economically distressed states immediate admission as **non-autonomous integration provinces**.

The normal Roman accession process preserves significant local self-government.

This new arrangement does not.

For the duration of integration, the incoming state temporarily surrenders much of its sovereignty to Roman institutions.

The standard integration period is set at **36 years**.

The Senate chooses thirty-six years because it is long enough to complete at least one full generation of:
- education reform
- transport construction
- industrial development
- public-health expansion
- administrative standardisation
- military integration

Yet it is short enough to be presented as temporary.

In practice, each treaty differs.

Some integration periods are as short as twenty-four years.

Others extend to fifty.

The harshest treaties include automatic extensions if repayment or security targets are not met.

## 1. The basic Roman terms

A state accepting the offer receives immediate assistance.

Rome provides:

- emergency grain and preserved food
- medical supplies
- water-treatment equipment
- railway and canal construction
- industrial machinery
- power infrastructure
- agricultural tools
- engineering personnel
- debt restructuring
- military protection
- reconstruction after environmental disasters

In return, the state becomes a Roman integration province.

It must accept:

- direct Roman fiscal supervision
- temporary restrictions on outward migration
- military-service quotas
- free transfer of land needed for approved industrial projects
- joint financing of the new infrastructure
- Latin Nova as an official language
- no more than one additional official provincial language
- Roman legal oversight
- common customs and trade regulations
- mandatory participation in resilience planning

The migration restrictions apply especially to movement into Rome’s older core provinces.

Residents may still travel for approved education, military service, technical work or family reunification, but mass settlement is temporarily controlled.

This satisfies the Civic Traditionalists, who want migration pressure reduced.

It also enrages many provincial inhabitants, who see the policy as collective confinement.

## 2. Industrial land grants

The most controversial term concerns land.

Any land designated for:

- factories
- rail corridors
- mines
- power stations
- storage depots
- industrial housing
- military logistics
- resilience infrastructure

must be transferred without purchase cost to the investing entity.

That entity may be:

- the Roman government
- a provincial authority
- a chartered industrial college
- a private Roman company
- a mixed public-private consortium
- a foreign partner approved by Rome

Existing inhabitants are entitled to relocation and compensation for structures or crops, but not for the underlying land itself.

In some provinces this produces orderly planned industrial zones.

In others it causes mass dispossession.

Corrupt officials deliberately classify fertile land as industrial land and transfer it to politically connected investors.

The Republic establishes special tribunals, but enforcement is uneven.

## 3. Joint financing

Rome does not pay for everything.

Each integration province must contribute to its own industrialisation through:

- taxation
- labour levies
- resource concessions
- provincial bonds
- future production pledges
- land grants
- local military construction

The poorer the state, the more of its contribution is made through labour and future output rather than immediate money.

Critics call this debt-financed annexation.

Supporters argue that completely free aid would overwhelm the Republic and encourage dependency.

Both interpretations are partly correct.

## 4. The guaranteed production share

The most innovative part of the arrangement is the **Provincial Output Guarantee**.

For every major Roman-financed industrial installation, the central government purchases a treaty-defined share of its output.

Usually this is between **12% and 28%**.

During the integration period, that share is delivered free to the province where it was produced.

Depending on the factory, this may include:

- steel
- fertiliser
- medicine
- tools
- pumps
- rail components
- preserved food
- construction materials
- electrical equipment
- vehicles

The programme gives poor provinces immediate access to products they otherwise could not afford.

After integration ends, the free allocation becomes a long-term loan obligation.

The province begins repaying the Roman treasury, with interest, over periods ranging from sixty to one hundred and twenty years.

The interest is deliberately low.

Repayments are indexed partly to provincial income so that a bad harvest or recession does not trigger immediate default.

The arrangement is generous enough to accelerate development and strict enough to bind the province to Rome for generations.

## 5. Language rules

Latin Nova becomes compulsory in:

- administration
- courts
- military command
- higher education
- engineering documentation
- interprovincial trade

Each province may recognise only one additional official language.

Other languages remain legal in private life, religion, local culture and primary education, but they receive no guaranteed state support.

Rome presents this as administrative necessity.

Opponents call it cultural compression.

In multilingual states, the requirement causes bitter disputes over which local language will receive official status.

Some governments choose the majority language.

Others choose a trade language.

A few deliberately choose no second official language to avoid internal conflict.

## 6. Military quotas

Every integration province must supply troops.

The standard quota is calculated from:

- population
- food surplus
- industrial capacity
- border exposure
- existing armed forces
- level of Roman subsidy

The troops are not merely local auxiliaries.

They are incorporated into Roman command structures.

Officer training occurs in Latin Nova.

Units are often deployed away from their home provinces to reduce the risk of rebellion.

For Rome, this is essential.

The Republic needs manpower urgently.

For the new provinces, military service offers:

- pay
- food
- citizenship
- technical education
- social mobility

But it also removes large numbers of young adults from already fragile communities.

## 7. The political bargain inside Rome

The integration policy survives because it offers something to every major Roman faction.

The Restorationists gain territorial expansion and a path toward planetary unity.

The Civic Traditionalists gain temporary migration restrictions protecting the core provinces.

The Industrial Front gains access to cheap land, labour, resources and large subsidised construction programmes.

The Resilience Coalition gains investment in neglected regions, reducing the pressure driving migration into already overloaded areas.

The military gains recruits.

The provincial administrations gain food and machinery.

No faction likes the entire package.

Every faction likes enough of it to vote yes.

## 8. The first states to accept

The first wave consists of states facing immediate collapse.

They include:

- drought-stricken inland republics
- heavily indebted coastal states
- former march territories
- fragmented river-basin confederations
- regions damaged by mining and soil exhaustion
- governments threatened by both famine and insurgency

Several sign within months.

Others negotiate fiercely.

Rome offers better terms to strategically important states.

A state controlling a major river, railway corridor or mineral basin may receive:

- a shorter integration period
- lower repayment interest
- reduced troop quotas
- greater local judicial authority
- a larger guaranteed output share

States with little strategic value receive harsher terms.

The unequal treaties generate resentment almost immediately.

## 9. Provincial variations

No two treaties are identical.

One province may receive free grain for fifteen years but accept a forty-year military quota.

Another may retain its monarch as ceremonial governor.

A third may preserve local courts but surrender its railway authority.

A fourth may receive full Roman citizenship immediately.

A fifth may have citizenship delayed until the twentieth year of integration.

Several governments insist on constitutional referenda.

Others sign without popular approval.

In some states, crowds celebrate Roman entry.

In others, Roman engineers arrive under military guard.

## 10. States that refuse Rome

A number of poor states reject the offer.

Their reasons vary.

Some fear permanent loss of sovereignty.

Some reject the language conditions.

Some distrust Roman debt.

Some have powerful local elites who would lose control over land and taxation.

Some believe the migration crisis will pass without surrendering autonomy.

Several instead negotiate with Rome for limited aid without integration.

At first, many appear doomed.

Yet as neighbouring integrated provinces stabilise, trade resumes, refugee flows decline and regional food systems recover.

A surprising number of refusing states eventually fare reasonably well.

They benefit indirectly from:

- new Roman rail links
- regional disease control
- reduced warfare
- cheaper manufactured goods
- stabilised markets
- employment across nearby borders

These states become evidence that integration was not the only possible route to survival.

Rome points out that their recovery would not have occurred without Roman intervention nearby.

The refusing states reply that they preserved their independence while receiving the same regional benefits.

The debate remains unresolved.

## 11. The rivals respond

The Eastern Mandate and Atlantic League initially dismiss Rome’s programme as desperate imperial overreach.

Within two years, they reverse course.

The Mandate offers distressed states:

- central planning assistance
- food guarantees
- military protection
- state-owned factories
- railway construction
- administrative integration

Its agreements are usually more authoritarian than Rome’s.

Local elites retain little power.

Migration is tightly controlled.

Military quotas are higher.

But the Mandate often pays a greater share of initial construction costs.

The Atlantic League offers:

- debt relief
- commercial port development
- industrial concessions
- shipping access
- private investment
- security guarantees

Its agreements preserve more formal sovereignty.

However, the participating states must accept long-term commercial monopolies and extensive creditor rights.

Both rivals are late.

Rome has already signed many of the most strategically important states.

The Mandate and League still secure several valuable partners, especially where hostility to Rome is strong.

## 12. The first effects

During the first five years, Rome’s programme appears disastrous.

The Republic spends enormous sums on:

- food
- railways
- military occupation
- disease control
- reconstruction
- industrial equipment

Roman taxes rise.

Government borrowing expands.

Core provinces complain that their wealth is being transferred outward.

The new provinces are not yet productive enough to repay the investment.

Industrialists profit from contracts, fuelling accusations that the programme is simply a transfer of public money to private interests.

Roman elections become increasingly violent.

## 13. Migration begins to slow

Despite the controversy, migration pressure begins declining by **117,116**.

The new provinces now have:

- guaranteed food imports
- paid construction work
- military wages
- expanding factories
- improving sanitation
- new irrigation systems
- local access to industrial output

People who would have migrated to Roman core cities remain home.

Some earlier migrants return to work on new projects.

The effect is uneven, but significant.

Rome’s urban crisis eases slightly.

Frontier refugee camps begin shrinking.

The Civic Traditionalists declare the policy successful.

Humanitarian critics reply that Rome reduced migration partly by restricting people’s movement.

## 14. The military transformation

The troop quotas create dozens of new Roman legions.

At first, their quality is poor.

Many recruits do not speak Latin Nova.

Equipment varies wildly.

Some units lack experienced officers.

Over the next decade, however, Roman training systems transform them.

New provincial legions become particularly skilled in:

- railway construction
- field engineering
- logistics
- river crossing
- disaster response
- irregular warfare
- operating in harsh climates

They know their home regions better than Rome’s older armies.

This later becomes decisive.

## 15. War becomes unavoidable

Between **117,114 and 117,118**, tensions intensify across all major alliances.

The Eastern Mandate fears that Rome’s integration programme will surround it with Roman provinces.

The Atlantic League fears that Roman industrial expansion will destroy its commercial dominance.

Rome fears that, without continued expansion, its new provinces will remain economically vulnerable and militarily exposed.

The Pacific states become the focus of escalating espionage and armed intervention.

Every major power accelerates mobilisation.

## 16. The ignition point

The Unification War begins on **17 May 117,119**.

The immediate cause is a crisis in the **Darian Corridor**, a strategically vital region linking Roman-associated territories to several newly integrated provinces.

The corridor contains:

- rail lines
- oil-like chemical feedstock deposits
- copper mines
- river crossings
- a major rocket-component factory

A local state had negotiated with both Rome and the Eastern Mandate.

Its government finally signs a Roman integration treaty.

A Mandate-backed military faction rejects the decision and seizes the capital.

Roman troops enter to restore the treaty government.

Mandate forces cross the eastern border, officially to protect local independence.

Within forty-eight hours, both sides are fighting openly.

Alliance obligations activate.

The war spreads faster than either side expects.

## 17. The name “Unification War”

At first, each side uses a different name.

Rome calls it the **War of Republican Defence**.

The Mandate calls it the **War Against Roman Encirclement**.

The Atlantic League calls it the **Continental Crisis**.

Provincial revolutionaries call it the **War of Liberation**.

Only later does “Unification War” become the common term.

The name reflects the deeper reality.

Every major power claims that Terra can no longer survive divided.

They disagree only over who should unify it and under what political order.

## 18. The first year

The Mandate advances rapidly.

Its armies are larger, more centralised and already positioned near the frontier.

Roman forces lose several major cities.

The Darian Corridor is cut in two.

Mandate rockets destroy Roman rail hubs.

Roman satellites are attacked.

The Atlantic League declares neutrality but begins supplying both sides selectively.

Rome’s generals fear a complete collapse of the eastern front.

The newly integrated provinces are still considered politically unreliable.

Instead, they become Rome’s first major surprise.

## 19. The provincial mobilisation

The integration provinces supply hundreds of thousands of soldiers.

Their railway and road projects, originally designed for industrial development, become military supply routes.

Factories built under the Provincial Output Guarantee begin producing:

- steel
- uniforms
- medical supplies
- rail components
- artillery
- vehicles
- preserved food

Because the Roman government already purchases part of their output, the conversion to military supply is rapid.

The local free-output shares also prevent immediate civilian collapse.

Provinces retain enough fertiliser, tools and transport equipment to maintain agriculture while supplying the army.

Without this system, Rome would have had to choose between feeding the front and feeding the new provinces.

It does not have to make that choice immediately.

## 20. The first Roman counterattack

In early **117,120**, provincial legions launch a counteroffensive through terrain the Mandate considered impassable.

They repair abandoned local railways, use animal transport where engines fail and bypass heavily defended industrial corridors.

Roman forces retake several cities.

The Mandate’s initial breakthrough is halted.

Rome does not recover all lost territory, but the front stabilises.

This is the first moment when the integration gamble proves militarily essential.

## 21. The Atlantic entry

The Atlantic League remains formally neutral for less than two years.

Roman naval inspections disrupt League trade.

Mandate submarines and private raiders attack League shipping.

League member states divide over which side poses the greater threat.

In **117,121**, a League convoy is destroyed in disputed waters.

Rome blames the Mandate.

The Mandate claims Roman aircraft carried out the attack to force League entry.

The surviving evidence is inconclusive.

The Atlantic League enters the war against the Mandate—but refuses to become Rome’s subordinate ally.

It declares a separate war with separate objectives.

The conflict now has three major power centres rather than two clear sides.

## 22. The League’s war aims

The Atlantic League seeks to:

- preserve maritime trade
- prevent either Rome or the Mandate from dominating Terra
- secure access to Pacific launch zones
- protect commercial treaty states
- establish international control over the Moon
- weaken Roman customs barriers

It cooperates with Rome on some fronts.

On others, Roman and League forces nearly fight each other.

The alliance is temporary, transactional and unstable.

## 23. The first space-race revival

War initially accelerates the space race.

Rockets become essential for:

- reconnaissance
- communication
- weather observation
- long-range weapons
- navigation
- propaganda

All major powers launch new satellites.

Rome announces that victory will be followed by a crewed lunar mission.

The Mandate claims it will recover the Moon first.

The Atlantic League proposes an international lunar authority under League management.

Space budgets reach their highest level in history.

For several years, the race intensifies dramatically.

## 24. The orbital battles

No side has sophisticated orbital weapons, but satellites are attacked constantly.

Methods include:

- interceptor rockets
- signal jamming
- sabotage of ground stations
- orbital collision attempts
- false navigation commands
- explosive charges hidden before launch

The first deliberate satellite-to-satellite collision occurs in **117,123**.

It produces a debris cloud that destroys two unrelated civilian satellites.

Weather forecasting deteriorates.

Military commanders lose reliable communication.

The damage affects every side.

Yet attacks continue.

## 25. The first lunar probe successes

In **117,124**, Rome successfully places an uncrewed probe into lunar orbit.

It transmits detailed images of ancient lunar settlements.

The images show:

- intact buried entrances
- collapsed surface towers
- preserved landing fields
- sealed industrial complexes
- areas of possible residual pressure

The achievement is celebrated across the Republic.

Within months, the Mandate sends its own probe.

The Atlantic League follows.

All three gather valuable information.

None can yet land reliably.

Each accuses the others of interfering with its signals.

## 26. The Southern Revolt

Rome’s war mobilisation places heavy pressure on the integration provinces.

Some provinces had accepted military quotas expecting limited service.

Now their troops are sent into a planetary war.

In **117,124**, several southern provinces revolt.

The rebels accuse Rome of:

- conscripting their youth
- transferring land to foreign industrialists
- imposing Latin
- controlling migration
- extracting resources
- delaying promised autonomy

The revolt threatens Roman supply lines.

The Mandate supplies some rebel factions.

The Atlantic League supplies others, hoping to weaken Rome without strengthening the Mandate.

Rome must divert forces from the main front.

## 27. The Roman response to revolt

Rome uses both repression and concessions.

Military forces retake major rail centres.

Rebel leaders responsible for massacres are executed.

At the same time, the Senate revises several integration treaties.

It offers:

- earlier provincial elections
- reduced interest rates
- stronger compensation for displaced landholders
- greater use of local languages in primary education
- lower troop quotas after specified service thresholds
- provincial ownership stakes in industrial projects

Some rebels accept.

Others continue fighting.

The revolt gradually fragments.

The reforms prevent it from becoming a general collapse.

## 28. The Mandate’s great western offensive

In **117,126**, the Eastern Mandate launches the largest offensive of the war so far.

It concentrates troops, artillery, rockets and armoured rail units across three fronts.

The offensive breaks through Roman defensive lines.

Several newly integrated provinces are occupied.

Mandate propaganda promises liberation from Roman debt and language rules.

Some local populations welcome the invaders.

Others resist, fearing Mandate centralisation.

Rome loses hundreds of thousands of soldiers and vast quantities of equipment.

For several months, defeat again appears likely.

## 29. The free-output system becomes a lifeline

The occupied provinces can no longer supply Rome, but the unoccupied integration provinces dramatically increase production.

The Roman government activates emergency clauses in their treaties.

Factories redirect output toward:

- ammunition
- replacement rails
- pumps
- medical supplies
- food processing
- bridge components
- uniforms

Because the provincial population continues receiving its guaranteed share, civilian support does not collapse as quickly as military planners expected.

The system is expensive.

Rome pays for goods it immediately distributes.

The treasury approaches insolvency.

But the armies remain supplied.

Without the integration network, Rome’s eastern armies would likely disintegrate during the winter of 117,126–117,127.

## 30. The Atlantic betrayal scare

In early **117,127**, League troops occupy several strategic islands and ports previously administered jointly with Rome.

The League claims the action is temporary and necessary for naval defence.

Rome interprets it as opportunistic expansion.

Roman and League ships exchange fire in one harbour.

For six days, it appears the League may switch sides or begin fighting both powers openly.

A compromise is reached.

The League keeps temporary control of the ports but agrees to shared administration.

The incident permanently damages trust.

## 31. The war in poor regions

The war is not confined to major front lines.

Poor states that rejected all integration agreements become battlegrounds.

Some try to remain neutral.

Others are invaded for:

- rail access
- food
- minerals
- river crossings
- launch sites

Several independent states form the **Neutral Survival Bloc**.

They coordinate border defence and humanitarian relief.

Because neighbouring Roman, Mandate and League-integrated regions are becoming more stable, the Bloc’s internal refugee pressures are lower than expected.

It survives by trading selectively with all sides.

Some of its members prosper from wartime commerce.

Others are devastated by proxy wars.

## 32. Migration during the war

The Roman integration policy reduces economic migration into core provinces, but war produces a new wave of refugees.

Millions flee:

- occupied territories
- bombed cities
- scorched agricultural zones
- rebel provinces
- collapsing border states

Rome attempts to direct refugees into planned settlement zones within integration provinces.

This is more sustainable than concentrating them in the old core.

However, local populations often resent being required to accept newcomers while their own rights remain restricted.

Violence breaks out in several provinces.

Rome deploys civic mediators, military police and emergency food teams.

Results vary widely.

## 33. The first strategic rocket attacks

By **117,128**, all major powers possess long-range rockets capable of striking distant cities.

Their accuracy is poor.

Their psychological effect is immense.

The Mandate attacks Roman rail centres and launch sites.

Rome strikes Mandate industrial districts.

The Atlantic League targets coastal military bases.

The war does not become a campaign of total city destruction, partly because the weapons are too unreliable and expensive.

But several rockets miss their targets and hit civilian districts.

Tens of thousands die.

Public demands for retaliation grow.

## 34. The chemical escalation

Industrial chemistry advances under wartime pressure.

All sides develop new explosives, propellants and toxic agents.

Several armies possess chemical weapons.

For years, they avoid large-scale use because weather prediction is unreliable and retaliation is feared.

In **117,129**, chemical agents are used during a siege.

Each side blames the other for the first release.

The gas drifts into civilian districts.

The deaths shock Terra.

Rome, the Mandate and the League negotiate a limited prohibition.

The agreement reduces use but does not eliminate it.

Smaller factions continue deploying chemical agents.

## 35. Rome’s northern advance

In **117,130**, Rome launches a major counteroffensive with League naval support.

The operation combines:

- provincial infantry
- Roman heavy artillery
- airships
- limited fixed-wing aircraft
- armoured rail units
- rocket reconnaissance
- local partisan networks

Roman forces retake much of the territory lost in the Mandate offensive.

They cross into Mandate-aligned lands.

For the first time, Rome appears capable of winning.

The advance outruns its supply systems.

Mandate forces destroy key bridges and rail junctions.

A harsh winter follows.

Roman troops become isolated.

The Mandate counterattacks.

Much of the captured territory is lost again.

## 36. The armies learn resilience warfare

The IRF makes high-energy systems unreliable even at Terra’s reduced intensity.

War accelerates the development of layered logistics.

Every major army now combines:

- motor vehicles
- railways
- animal transport
- bicycles
- river barges
- manual carts
- radio
- wired telegraph
- signal flags
- optical towers
- powered pumps
- hand pumps

The side with the most advanced machines does not always win.

The side that can continue functioning after machines fail often does.

Rome’s long commitment to backup systems gives it an advantage.

The Mandate adapts quickly.

The League remains highly effective near ports but struggles inland.

## 37. The second space-race surge

The Roman lunar-orbit success and the war’s propaganda needs trigger a second surge around **117,130–117,132**.

Rome begins assembling a crewed lunar-flyby vehicle.

The Mandate announces a landing programme.

The Atlantic League funds two competing commercial lunar missions.

Pilot training expands.

Large launch vehicles are tested.

For a brief period, a human lunar mission seems possible within a decade.

Then sabotage intensifies.

## 38. The destruction of Portus Caeli

In **117,132**, Rome’s largest launch complex, **Portus Caeli**, is destroyed by a chain of explosions.

The blast kills thousands of engineers, soldiers and workers.

Three nearly completed launch vehicles are lost.

The attack’s origin remains disputed.

Evidence points to:

- Mandate intelligence
- anti-space militants
- a Roman separatist group
- corrupt contractors concealing defects

The investigation never reaches a definitive conclusion.

Rome’s lunar programme is delayed by years.

## 39. The Mandate launch disaster

Later the same year, a Mandate heavy rocket explodes during fuelling.

The blast destroys the central assembly hall and damages nearby industrial districts.

Official reports claim an accident.

Foreign intelligence suggests League sabotage.

The Mandate executes several engineers and security officers.

This produces fear throughout its scientific institutions.

Innovation slows because technicians become reluctant to report uncertain results.

## 40. The League’s financial collapse

The Atlantic League’s lunar programme depends heavily on private finance.

War losses, shipping disruption and failed launches trigger a financial panic in **117,133**.

Several major launch corporations collapse.

Banks holding lunar securities fail.

The League government nationalises key aerospace firms.

Public anger rises over the cost.

The League continues military satellite launches but sharply reduces crewed lunar plans.

This marks the beginning of the space race’s decline.

## 41. The Pacific reversal

The Pacific Cavity states initially benefit from selling launch access and technology to every major power.

The war eventually reaches them directly.

In **117,133**, a coalition backed by the Mandate attacks Roman-aligned launch territories.

League-backed states intervene.

Rome sends expeditionary forces.

The Pacific region fragments into a complex multi-sided war.

Launch sites are seized, recaptured and destroyed.

The world’s best spaceflight environment becomes one of its most heavily bombed regions.

Rocket development suffers a devastating setback.

## 42. The Third Front

While Rome and the Mandate fight across the main continental front, the Atlantic League launches operations against Mandate-aligned coastal states.

Its navies advance rapidly.

League marines capture major ports.

Local populations initially welcome the removal of Mandate administrators.

They then resist League commercial controls.

Insurgencies spread.

The League wins territory but cannot govern it cheaply.

Its forces become trapped in expensive occupations.

## 43. The Mandate’s internal crisis

By **117,134**, the Mandate faces severe strain.

Central planning keeps its armies supplied, but civilian shortages grow.

Food rationing intensifies.

Peripheral populations resent conscription.

Several industrial regions experience strikes and sabotage.

A group of senior officers attempts to remove part of the civilian leadership.

The coup fails.

Purges follow.

For several months, Mandate operations become disorganised.

Rome attempts to exploit the crisis.

## 44. Rome’s deepest advance

In **117,135**, Roman armies penetrate farther into Mandate territory than ever before.

New provincial legions lead the advance.

They perform exceptionally well.

Many soldiers come from regions that had been impoverished only twenty years earlier.

Their families now depend on Roman factories, food guarantees and military wages.

Their loyalty is not necessarily ideological.

It is practical.

Rome has tied their survival to the Republic’s survival.

The advance captures major industrial centres.

Mandate forces retreat.

Some observers predict the war will end within two years.

They are wrong.

## 45. The winter counterstroke

The Mandate deliberately abandons several cities and destroys their infrastructure.

Roman armies move into devastated regions.

Supply lines lengthen.

The winter of **117,135–117,136** is unusually severe across the central front.

Roman fuel systems fail.

Rail lines freeze or are sabotaged.

Disease spreads.

Mandate forces, operating from prepared low-energy supply depots, counterattack.

Rome loses much of its advance.

Hundreds of thousands are captured.

The Republic suffers one of the worst defeats in its history.

## 46. The Roman treasury crisis

By **117,136**, Rome is close to financial collapse.

It is paying for:

- the war
- provincial food guarantees
- industrial construction
- refugee relief
- debt servicing
- military pensions
- destroyed infrastructure
- the remnants of the space programme

Several senators demand suspension of the Provincial Output Guarantee.

The integration provinces warn that ending it would cause famine and rebellion.

The Senate instead issues long-term reconstruction bonds and imposes emergency taxes on industrial profits.

The Industrial Front resists but ultimately accepts.

It understands that a Roman defeat would destroy the legal order protecting its investments.

## 47. Why integration saves Rome

The integration programme now proves its full value.

The new provinces provide Rome with four things it cannot replace.

First, they provide soldiers.

By 117,136, a large portion of the Roman army comes from integration provinces.

Second, they provide industrial depth.

Factories are spread across many regions rather than concentrated in the old Roman core.

Mandate rockets cannot destroy them all.

Third, they provide food.

Irrigation, fertiliser and transport investments made before the war have turned several formerly poor regions into major agricultural suppliers.

Fourth, they reduce internal migration pressure.

Without them, tens of millions more displaced people would have entered Rome’s core cities during wartime.

The Republic would likely have faced famine, urban revolt and military collapse simultaneously.

The policy that nearly bankrupted Rome becomes the structure that keeps it alive.

## 48. The autonomy controversy

The integration period was supposed to be temporary.

Several provinces approach scheduled autonomy milestones during the war.

Rome postpones some transitions on security grounds.

Provincial leaders accuse the Republic of breaking its promises.

The Senate argues that autonomous governments cannot be created while front lines cross their territories.

Some provinces accept the delay.

Others revolt.

Rome faces a dangerous choice between military necessity and constitutional legitimacy.

It adopts a compromise.

Provincial elections proceed, but defence, finance and strategic industry remain under temporary Roman control.

The arrangement satisfies few people but prevents a broader rebellion.

## 49. The Latin question

The language requirement has mixed results.

Latin Nova improves military coordination and administration.

It allows engineers and officials from dozens of regions to work together.

It also produces cultural resistance.

Several underground movements deliberately revive suppressed regional languages.

Schools become political battlegrounds.

Rome gradually relaxes the rules.

Latin remains compulsory for central administration, but provinces gain greater freedom to fund additional languages locally.

This reduces resistance without abandoning the common administrative language.

## 50. The war turns maritime

By **117,137**, the continental fronts are exhausted.

The Atlantic League attempts to exploit this by tightening control over global shipping.

It imposes new inspection regimes and wartime fees.

Rome refuses.

Mandate-aligned raiders attack League shipping.

Roman and League naval units begin confronting each other again despite their nominal cooperation.

The war becomes increasingly multipolar.

There is no longer a clear division between allies and enemies.

## 51. The League-Roman clash

In late **117,137**, a Roman convoy carrying grain from integration provinces refuses League inspection.

League ships attempt to stop it.

Roman escorts fire warning shots.

A League vessel is hit.

The confrontation escalates into a two-day naval battle.

Both governments claim the incident was accidental.

Neither declares war on the other.

But military cooperation nearly ends.

The Mandate benefits immediately.

## 52. The peace initiative

Neutral states propose a general peace conference in **117,138**.

The conference addresses:

- frontiers
- migration
- provincial sovereignty
- lunar access
- space demilitarisation
- trade routes
- chemical weapons
- refugee return

All major powers attend.

None is willing to compromise enough.

Rome demands recognition of its integration treaties.

The Mandate demands withdrawal of Roman forces from disputed provinces.

The League demands open trade and international control of launch sites.

The negotiations collapse after an assassination attempt against a Mandate delegate.

The attacker’s affiliation remains unclear.

## 53. The third space-race intensification

The failed peace conference produces one final burst of lunar competition.

Each power fears that the others will use negotiations to gain time.

Rome attempts to restore a crewed flyby programme.

The Mandate moves a heavy rocket to a hidden launch facility.

The League finances an emergency multinational mission.

For eighteen months, lunar plans again receive enormous resources.

Then the material shortage becomes impossible to ignore.

## 54. Resource exhaustion

The war consumes the same resources needed for spaceflight:

- high-grade steel
- aluminium-like light alloys
- precision bearings
- pure chemical fuels
- optical glass
- trained engineers
- electrical relays
- testing facilities

Launch vehicles compete directly with:

- rockets
- aircraft
- field communication
- rail repair
- artillery
- medical production

Governments choose the war.

Space programmes shrink.

Some continue as propaganda projects.

None has the capacity for a serious crewed lunar mission.

## 55. The decline of the space race

By **117,139**, most crewed lunar programmes are formally suspended.

Orbital military launches continue.

Small scientific satellites still operate.

Lunar probes become rare.

Launch complexes are converted to weapons production.

Astronauts are reassigned as pilots, engineers or officers.

The Moon remains central to propaganda but peripheral to actual budgets.

Humanity has come closer to reaching it than ever before.

War pulls it back.

## 56. The Great Provincial Offensive

Rome begins a major new campaign in spring **117,139**.

The operation is planned and supplied largely through the integration provinces.

It uses:

- provincial legions
- local river transport
- distributed factories
- mobile repair units
- refugee labour corps
- low-energy fallback logistics

Instead of one massive thrust, Rome attacks across numerous smaller fronts.

The strategy prevents the Mandate from concentrating its forces.

Roman armies recover several important territories.

Some occupied provinces rise against Mandate rule.

The offensive does not break the enemy, but it restores Rome’s strategic position.

## 57. The Mandate’s eastern victory

At the same time, the Mandate defeats a League-backed coalition on another front.

It captures several commercial cities and gains access to new resource regions.

The victory compensates for its losses against Rome.

The Mandate remains fully capable of continuing the war.

Its propaganda declares that Rome’s offensive is a final desperate act.

Roman propaganda claims the Mandate is collapsing.

Neither claim is true.

## 58. The League’s colonial revolts

The Atlantic League’s weaker treaty states begin revolting against wartime requisitions.

The League had promised investment and sovereignty.

Instead, many states receive military occupation and commercial extraction.

Several revolts cut important supply routes.

League armies suppress some.

Others negotiate improved terms.

The League’s global position weakens.

It remains powerful at sea but increasingly unstable on land.

## 59. The state of the war in year 117,140

Thirty years after Rome’s integration gamble, and twenty-one years after the war began, no side has won.

Rome has:

- survived repeated near-defeats
- expanded its industrial base
- integrated millions of new citizens and soldiers
- recovered much of its lost territory
- suffered enormous casualties
- accumulated vast debt
- become more centralised and militarised

The Eastern Mandate has:

- captured and lost major regions
- preserved its core industrial strength
- endured internal purges and shortages
- expanded in some theatres
- been pushed back in others
- retained one of the world’s largest armies

The Atlantic League has:

- won control of key maritime zones
- lost commercial stability
- occupied important ports
- faced colonial revolts
- weakened both Rome and the Mandate
- failed to impose a lasting balance

The independent states have:

- survived through neutrality, regional cooperation or opportunistic trade
- benefited from stabilisation around integrated regions
- suffered invasions where strategically exposed
- demonstrated that refusal of all great-power offers did not always mean collapse

## 60. Casualties and displacement

By year 117,140, the war has killed tens of millions directly.

Far more have died from:

- famine
- disease
- infrastructure collapse
- displacement
- chemical exposure
- interrupted medicine
- winter shortages

Hundreds of millions have moved at least once.

Some migration routes have become permanent settlement corridors.

Entire regions have changed language, religion and political identity.

The distinction between refugee, migrant, soldier and settler becomes increasingly blurred.

## 61. Rome’s gamble in retrospect

The integration policy remains deeply controversial.

It caused:

- debt
- cultural conflict
- land seizures
- military conscription
- administrative centralisation
- provincial revolts

It also produced:

- food security
- new factories
- irrigation
- railways
- military manpower
- distributed industrial capacity
- reduced migration pressure
- regional recovery

Without it, Rome likely would have lost the war’s opening phase.

Its core cities would have faced even greater migration.

Its armies would have lacked replacements.

Its industry would have remained too concentrated.

Its food supplies would have failed during the great Mandate offensives.

The policy does not make Rome victorious.

It makes Rome survivable.

## 62. The final scene of the period

On the night of **31 December 117,140**, Roman observers track a surviving lunar probe as it crosses the dark face of the Moon.

Its transmitter is weak.

Its orbit is decaying.

Below, Terra is covered by railway lights, blackout zones, burning frontiers and refugee camps.

The Moon still contains the ruins of humanity’s former glory.

No one can reach them.

Rome is stronger than it was thirty years earlier, but also more indebted, more militarised and less certain of its republican character.

The Mandate is wounded but unbroken.

The Atlantic League is divided but still dominant at sea.

The integration provinces, once considered burdens, have become the factories, farms and armies keeping Rome alive.

The Unification War has not unified anything yet.

It has only proven that no major power can easily destroy the others.

And after twenty-one years of war, every side still believes one more offensive might finally decide the future of Terra.
