# 117400-117500 — The Century of Redundancy

The field continues declining at the established rate.

Terra’s average IRF intensity falls from approximately:

- 57.1% in 117,400
- 56.6% in 117,450
- 56.1% in 117,500

Approximate intensity by year 117,500:

| Location | IRF intensity |
| --- | --- |
| Wound of Return | 46–49% |
| Terra average | 56.1% |
| Near-Terran orbit | 58–61% |
| Luna | 63–67% |
| Mars | 81–87% |
| Asteroid belt | 90–95% |
| Jupiter and Europa | 97–100% |
| Saturn and Titan | ~100% |
| Novaterra | 100% |
| Beyond the expanding frontier | 0% |

The next scheduled burst remains due in **118,110**.

The decline is gradual, but the difference between Terra and the outer worlds is becoming increasingly important.

Terra can now support technologies that remain impossible on Mars and entirely impractical around Jupiter or Saturn.

---

## 1. The Doctrine of Descending Technology

In **117,404**, the Senate establishes the **Programme for Descending Technology**.

Its purpose is to design systems that can continue functioning as the IRF strengthens.

Every major device is to be classified according to the highest IRF intensity under which it remains reliable.

The official classes are:

- Class 50 — reliable at 50% IRF
- Class 60
- Class 75
- Class 90
- Class 100 — reliable under full pre-industrial IRF intensity

Class 100 becomes the programme’s ultimate objective.

A Class 100 device must operate using:

- low energy density
- moderate pressure
- low rotational speed
- simple electrical loads
- mechanical regulation
- repairable materials
- minimal precision manufacturing
- local power sources

The programme does not reject advanced technology.

It tries to rebuild industrial functions in forms that remain physically stable under stronger IRF conditions.

---

## 2. Low-energy industrialisation

Roman engineers begin redesigning industrial processes.

They study whether the functions of a factory can be divided among many smaller machines rather than concentrated into one high-energy installation.

Examples include:

## Distributed steelmaking

Instead of one enormous furnace, a region uses many smaller furnaces linked by transport and quality-control systems.

## Low-pressure chemical production

Processes operate slowly in larger vessels rather than rapidly under extreme pressure.

## Mechanical computation

Tasks normally assigned to high-density electronics are distributed among relay rooms, optical systems and mechanical memory.

## Local electrical grids

Cities are divided into many independently powered districts rather than relying on one high-output network.

## Modular water systems

Gravity, reservoirs and hand-operated backups reduce dependence on large pumps.

## Slow manufacturing

Machines operate at lower speed for longer periods, sacrificing output per hour for reliability.

These systems are less efficient by conventional industrial standards.

They are far more resilient.

---

## 3. The Class 100 challenge

Not every industrial function can be reproduced at full-strength IRF.

Class 100 technology can sustain:

- water treatment
- local electricity
- printing
- mechanical calculation
- radio at modest power
- food processing
- low-pressure steam
- basic chemical medicine
- precision craftsmanship
- railway signalling
- small-scale metallurgy
- simple refrigeration

It cannot yet reproduce:

- large modern aircraft
- semiconductor fabrication
- nuclear energy
- fusion
- powerful rocketry
- high-density computing
- large synthetic-material industries
- global power grids

Roman engineers therefore distinguish between:

- permanent technologies, which can survive full IRF
- conditional technologies, which depend on weaker conditions
- recovery technologies, which can help rebuild conditional systems after a collapse

This classification becomes mandatory in Roman infrastructure planning.

---

## 4. The Library Mandate

Every design produced under the Descending Technology Programme must be stored widely.

Copies are placed in:

- every Great Library
- provincial technical libraries
- major universities
- military engineering archives
- lunar repositories
- selected independent-state archives

Designs are preserved in several forms:

- printed manuals
- engraved metal
- ceramic plates
- microphotographic records
- magnetic storage
- optical storage
- mechanical models

The most important instructions are illustrated so they can be understood even if language or technical education declines.

Every design includes versions for different capability levels.

A water system, for example, may have:

- a modern electrically regulated form
- a low-voltage form
- a mechanical form
- a gravity-fed emergency form
- a manually operated final fallback

---

## 5. Long Age engineering

Rome discovers that the Long Age contains far more than historical curiosity.

Before the First Breaking, humanity had spent tens of thousands of years perfecting technologies that worked under full IRF intensity.

Long Age engineers had developed:

- extraordinarily efficient water systems
- low-friction mechanical transmission
- durable bearings
- low-pressure heat engines
- mechanical automation
- precision clockwork
- hydraulic computation
- passive climate control
- gravity-fed urban infrastructure
- long-lived architecture
- low-energy metallurgy

Many of these systems had been ignored during the Great Release because industrial machinery made them seem obsolete.

Rome now studies them as solutions to permanent survival.

---

## 6. What Rome still learns from the Long Age

Roman engineers initially assume they already understand the Long Age’s basic methods.

They are wrong.

Recovered manuals reveal techniques lost during industrial simplification.

These include:

- complex passive ventilation
- water networks regulated without powered valves
- buildings designed to maintain temperature through geometry
- mechanical systems that compensate automatically for wear
- modular machines repairable with local materials
- hydraulic logic systems
- precision gearing designed for centuries of operation
- urban drainage capable of functioning without central power

The Long Age did not lack sophistication.

It concentrated sophistication into design rather than energy.

This insight reshapes Roman engineering culture.

---

## 7. The New Artisanship

A cultural movement develops around the recovered Long Age aesthetic.

It becomes known as **Ars Continua**, the Continuous Art.

Its followers reject the idea that machinery should be visually disposable or purely functional.

They design devices in which:

- mechanisms are visible
- repairs are understandable
- decoration communicates purpose
- materials age gracefully
- components can be replaced individually
- beauty encourages preservation

Machines are decorated with:

- geometric engraving
- civic symbols
- botanical patterns
- historical scenes
- mathematical diagrams

A pumping station may also be a public monument.

A railway clock may contain carved histories of its city.

A mechanical calculator may be built as carefully as a musical instrument.

---

## 8. Monument restoration accelerates

Ars Continua strengthens the monument programme.

Restoration is no longer treated as separate from modern construction.

New buildings are designed to coexist with recovered ancient structures.

Roman cities begin unburying:

- impact-era shelters
- Long Age districts
- industrial underground transit systems
- ancient civic centres
- war-buried neighbourhoods

Some are restored as museums.

Others are resettled.

Buried cities often preserve structures better than surface ruins.

But excavation is slow and dangerous.

Hazards include:

- unstable foundations
- contaminated water
- trapped gases
- old explosives
- chemical waste
- collapsed tunnels

The work is expected to continue for centuries.

---

## 9. Latin becomes stricter

In **117,409**, the Institute of Perpetual Latin issues the **Public Language Standard**.

Permanent Latin becomes the only permitted form of Latin in:

- schools
- hospitals
- ministries
- police
- courts
- state media
- public universities
- the armed forces
- lunar institutions
- national transport systems

Public employees are required to use standard pronunciation and grammar while performing official duties.

Regional accents are tolerated, but vocabulary and syntax must remain compatible with the permanent form.

Daily speech outside public institutions continues changing naturally.

Slang develops.

Pronunciation shifts locally.

But every generation repeatedly encounters standard Latin through:

- education
- healthcare
- law
- media
- employment
- public announcements

This prevents everyday Latin from drifting into completely separate languages.

---

## 10. Is the language policy effective?

Over the century, the policy largely succeeds.

Spoken regional varieties develop, but they remain mutually intelligible.

A citizen may speak a local form at home and Permanent Latin in public life.

The two influence each other continuously.

The main costs are cultural and social.

Critics argue that:

- local speech is treated as inferior
- public employment favours standard speakers
- provincial accents attract discrimination
- language regulation suppresses creativity

Rome responds by funding local literature and broadcasting while keeping official Latin fixed.

The result is not complete linguistic uniformity.

It is stable bilingualism between formal Permanent Latin and local speech.

---

## 11. Latin beyond Terra

Communication with Mars becomes almost entirely Latin.

Martian settlements possess many local languages, but Latin provides a common interplanetary standard.

The Martian Covenant adopts Permanent Latin for:

- astronomical data
- scientific correspondence
- emergency communication
- archive exchange
- communication with Terra and Luna

Martian pronunciation differs strongly from Terran pronunciation because of isolation and physiology.

Written Latin remains compatible.

This marks the beginning of Latin as a Solar lingua franca.

Europa receives simplified Latin messages, though its own languages remain dominant.

Titanian scholars also begin studying it from received transmissions.

---

## 12. Expansion of Luna Restituta

Luna Restituta changes from a small resettlement project into a permanent archaeological and scientific programme.

In **117,412**, Rome adopts the **Lunar Continuity Plan**.

Its objectives are:

- establish multiple permanent stations
- maintain independent life-support loops
- recover archives systematically
- preserve lunar ruins
- build Class 100 fallback systems
- create emergency shelters capable of surviving stronger IRF conditions
- prepare Luna as a relay point for interplanetary communication

Luna remains dependent on Terra.

The plan aims to reduce the speed at which it would die if supplies stopped.

---

## 13. Roman standards on Luna

Lunar safety law is absolute.

Only Permanent Latin may be used for:

- commands
- technical manuals
- signage
- emergency procedures
- software interfaces
- equipment labels
- medical records

Only Roman national standards are permitted for:

- pressure fittings
- electrical connectors
- air systems
- measurements
- timekeeping
- tools
- rail systems
- clothing seals
- emergency symbols

No provincial variation is allowed.

The reason is practical.

A person in a failing habitat must not wonder which measurement system, language convention or connector standard is being used.

Luna becomes the most standardised human environment in existence.

---

## 14. Low-energy lunar systems

Lunar engineers adapt the Descending Technology Programme to vacuum habitats.

They develop:

- gravity-fed internal water loops where geometry permits
- manually operated pressure doors
- low-speed air circulation
- biological oxygen renewal
- mechanical carbon-dioxide scrubbers
- thermal storage in rock and water
- low-voltage lighting
- hand-repairable pumps
- passive emergency cooling
- pressure compartments small enough to isolate rapidly

True Class 100 self-sufficiency remains impossible on Luna.

The environment itself requires technology.

But Class 100-compatible internal systems can greatly prolong survival.

A lunar district that loses advanced power may continue operating in emergency mode for months or years rather than hours.

---

## 15. Lunar population growth

Luna’s population grows cautiously.

Approximate population:

- 6,000–9,000 in 117,400
- 18,000 by 117,425
- 45,000 by 117,450
- 90,000 by 117,475
- 140,000–180,000 by 117,500

The population remains tiny compared with ancient Luna.

Growth is limited by:

- transport costs
- debris risk
- food production
- life-support redundancy
- stronger local IRF
- psychological screening
- safety reviews

Lunar residency is treated as public service rather than unrestricted migration.

---

## 16. Lunar archaeology

Archaeological work expands into major underground cities.

Researchers reconstruct:

- the chronology of post-impact failures
- evacuation decisions
- political fragmentation
- agricultural collapse
- settlement isolation
- the final centuries of lunar life

They find records from communities that survived far longer than Terra had known.

Some maintained functioning schools, councils and farms centuries after interplanetary contact ended.

Others collapsed through:

- air loss
- water contamination
- civil war
- reproductive decline
- crop disease
- structural failure

Lunar history becomes much more detailed and much more tragic.

---

## 17. Reconstructing the death of Luna

By the 117,440s, Roman historians produce a near-continuous chronology from the Second Breaking to the final confirmed lunar death.

The history is assembled from:

- local council records
- personal diaries
- emergency logs
- agricultural reports
- radio fragments
- burial records
- mechanical system logs
- Terran observations
- Martian archives

The final large settlement did not vanish instantly.

It declined over generations.

Its last inhabitants understood that no rescue would come.

The last recorded message is not a plea.

It is an inventory of preserved knowledge and instructions for future visitors.

This message becomes mandatory reading in Roman schools.

---

## 18. The History of Dependence

Rome incorporates the reconstructed lunar history into a wider educational programme called **The History of Dependence**.

Every Roman student studies:

- the Second Breaking
- the return of the IRF
- the failure of industrial life support
- lunar extinction
- Martian contraction
- orbital habitat collapse
- the survival of Titan and Europa
- Terra’s ecological advantage
- the dangers of irreversible technological dependency

The lesson is not that technology is evil.

It is that no essential system should be allowed to fail completely when advanced machinery becomes unavailable.

Students learn both industrial and fallback methods.

---

## 19. Collaboration with Mars

Mars provides records unavailable on Terra or Luna.

Martian archives contain:

- observations of lunar radio decline
- records of refugee arrivals
- data on failing interplanetary transport
- correspondence with late lunar settlements
- technical manuals exchanged during the collapse
- accounts of Martian aid attempts

Rome and Mars create a shared historical commission.

Communication remains slow but increasingly reliable through lunar relays.

Latin becomes the commission’s working language.

The Martian version of events complicates Roman narratives.

It reveals that some Terran authorities withheld information during the collapse and prioritised politically favoured evacuations.

Rome includes these findings rather than suppressing them.

---

## 20. Communication with Europa

Direct communication with Europa remains difficult.

Its transmissions must pass through strong IRF regions and originate from weak surface systems.

Rome deploys listening stations on Luna and eventually sends primitive relay probes toward Jupiter.

During this century, only the earliest of these probes reach useful positions.

Communication improves modestly.

Europa can exchange:

- compressed text
- astronomical observations
- biological reports
- brief historical records

Long conversations remain impossible.

Messages may take months or years to assemble and confirm.

Permanent Latin is used on the Roman side.

Europans develop a simplified Latin technical register for external communication.

---

## 21. Communication with Titan

Titan is even more distant.

Its full-strength IRF environment severely limits transmission power.

Rome receives occasional Titanian signals through enormous receiving arrays.

Replies are uncertain.

The first Roman relay missions toward Saturn are launched during this century, but most will not arrive until later.

Communication remains sparse.

Still, Titanian archives begin receiving Permanent Latin reference texts, ensuring future compatibility.

---

## 22. The Permanent Relay Network

In **117,418**, Rome formally begins the **Permanent Solar Relay Network**.

The project aims to place low-energy communication relays near:

- Luna
- Mars
- the inner asteroid belt
- Jupiter
- Saturn
- eventually the outer Solar System

The relays are designed in two layers.

## Transit systems

These use the most advanced propulsion available under current IRF conditions.

They are allowed to be complex and conditional.

## Permanent relay cores

These are designed for the lowest possible energy use.

They rely on:

- low-power radio
- optical signalling
- slow data rates
- large passive antennas
- mechanical orientation systems
- durable clocks
- solar or thermal trickle charging
- redundant memory
- intermittent operation

The relay may wake only periodically, transmit a compressed message, then become dormant again.

---

## 23. Can a relay work at 100% IRF?

Yes, but only in limited form.

Full-strength IRF permits:

- small electrical currents
- simple radio
- mechanical clocks
- low-power computation
- local energy storage

A Class 100 relay cannot provide continuous high-bandwidth communication.

It can provide:

- slow text
- numerical data
- emergency beacons
- timing signals
- compressed images over long periods
- store-and-forward messaging

A message that modern Terra sends in seconds may require days or weeks.

At outer-system distances, confirmation may require months.

The network is therefore physically plausible but primitive.

Its value is resilience, not speed.

---

## 24. Early relay missions

During this century, Rome successfully establishes:

- multiple lunar relays
- several high-orbit Terran relays
- two relays along the Terra–Mars communication geometry
- a Martian orbital receiver
- one inner-belt beacon
- experimental solar-orbiting repeater stations

Rome also launches probes toward Jupiter and Saturn.

Their propulsion is slow.

The Jupiter missions require decades.

The Saturn missions require longer.

Some fail due to:

- navigation errors
- debris
- propulsion instability
- component degradation

The network is far from complete by 117,500.

Its foundations are real.

---

## 25. The global financial crisis

The financial crisis begins with a natural phenomenon known as the **Four Dark Summers**.

Between **117,427 and 117,431**, three large volcanic eruptions occur in different tropical and subpolar regions.

No single eruption is civilisation-ending.

Together they inject enough material into the atmosphere to cause:

- cooler summers
- irregular rainfall
- weaker monsoons
- repeated crop failures
- reduced solar output
- transport disruption
- livestock disease

At the same time, a strong solar magnetic cycle increases local IRF instability in several electrical and industrial regions.

The combination creates a global production shock.

---

## 26. Why the crisis becomes financial

Rome has invested heavily in projects with long-term benefits and little immediate return:

- Great Libraries
- lunar settlement
- monument restoration
- biosphere recovery
- foreign infrastructure
- population redistribution
- low-energy research
- relay probes

These projects are valuable but not quickly profitable.

Governments and banks assume steady tax growth will continue.

The harvest shocks reduce revenue precisely when food imports and emergency spending rise.

Several provincial development banks fail.

Investors panic.

Credit freezes.

International trade contracts.

The crisis spreads through every major economy.

---

## 27. Rome protects itself

Rome responds with strong internal protections.

It temporarily imposes:

- capital controls
- emergency food purchasing
- limits on speculative withdrawals
- state guarantees for household deposits
- export controls on grain
- public employment programmes
- temporary industrial subsidies
- debt moratoria for poor provinces

The Republic prioritises its own citizens and provinces.

Foreign investment is sharply reduced.

This decision is politically understandable and globally damaging.

Many independent states depend on Roman capital.

When investment stops, their economies collapse.

---

## 28. The civic floor proves its value

The incomplete universal income becomes one of Rome’s strongest stabilising systems.

The civic floor maintains purchasing power among ordinary citizens.

Even when unemployment rises, people can still buy:

- staple food
- basic housing
- water
- medicine
- local transport

This prevents the collapse of consumer demand.

Small businesses survive longer.

Mass homelessness does not explode as it did during earlier crises.

The civic floor does not prevent recession.

It prevents recession from becoming social disintegration.

---

## 29. Restoration programmes slow

Nearly every grand project faces cuts.

The most heavily affected are:

- monument restoration
- urban archaeological excavation
- biosphere reintroduction
- new Great Library construction
- external development partnerships

Projects already underway are preserved where possible.

New ones are delayed.

The Latin Preservation Programme continues because it is inexpensive relative to physical construction.

Descending Technology research also remains funded because leaders see it as directly relevant to crisis survival.

---

## 30. Lunar programmes during the crisis

Luna’s budget is cut, but not abandoned.

The Republic preserves funding for:

- life support
- emergency transport
- archaeology at priority sites
- relay construction
- debris monitoring
- biological vault recovery

Population growth slows.

Several planned settlements are postponed.

No lunar district is closed.

The crisis strengthens the argument that Luna must become more locally resilient.

Lunar engineers accelerate Class 100 emergency-system development.

---

## 31. The collapse of vulnerable states

Outside Rome, the Four Dark Summers produce severe crises.

Several poor independent states face:

- food shortages
- currency collapse
- debt default
- unemployment
- public unrest
- government failure

Some had believed they could maintain Roman-level living standards indefinitely while remaining independent.

The crisis exposes their dependence on Roman finance, medicine and trade.

Several governments collapse.

Others impose authoritarian emergency rule.

Millions request entry into Roman territory.

---

## 32. Accession is suspended

Independent states repeatedly request Roman accession.

Normally, Rome might consider them.

Instead, the Senate suspends new accessions in **117,430**.

The official reason is administrative capacity.

The Republic cannot absorb new territories while:

- stabilising its own economy
- protecting food supplies
- supporting Luna
- managing financial failures
- preserving the civic floor

Rome offers emergency aid and association treaties where possible.

Many applicants interpret the suspension as abandonment.

The policy is bitterly criticised but probably prevents the crisis from overwhelming Roman institutions.

---

## 33. The Atlantic League nears collapse

The League is hit harder than Rome.

Its economy depends heavily on:

- finance
- shipping
- investment flows
- imported food
- commercial confidence

The harvest shock reduces trade.

Roman capital controls cut League investment access.

Several League banks fail.

Member states accuse the central commercial councils of protecting wealthy ports at everyone else’s expense.

Secession movements grow.

For a time, the League appears likely to dissolve entirely.

---

## 34. The League chooses dependence over confrontation

Before the crisis, anti-Roman states had proposed a broad coalition including the Atlantic League.

The League declines.

Its leaders conclude that survival now depends on:

- access to Roman markets
- Roman grain
- Roman investment
- Roman medicines
- shared shipping standards

The League avoids antagonising Rome.

It accepts emergency trade agreements and limited Roman oversight of joint financial mechanisms.

This preserves the League.

It also confirms its secondary status.

---

## 35. The formation of the Serevan Compact

A coalition of independent states forms in **117,432**.

It calls itself the **Serevan Compact**.

The name comes from an invented legal term in one of its founding languages meaning roughly “standing apart without standing alone.”

The Compact is designed to:

- resist Roman political influence
- coordinate trade
- protect member sovereignty
- pool financial reserves
- establish common defence
- create independent technical standards
- reduce dependence on Roman institutions

Its founding members include inland republics, island states and former neutral federations.

They are not hostile enough to seek war.

They are determined not to become Roman satellites.

---

## 36. The Compact’s original vision

The Serevan Compact initially hopes to include:

- the Atlantic League
- former League secession states
- independent former Mandate republics
- neutral commercial cities

The League refuses to join.

Without League shipping and finance, the Compact is weaker than intended.

It remains a significant political bloc but cannot rival Rome economically.

Its leaders nevertheless use the Roman crisis to claim that dependence on one world power is dangerous.

---

## 37. Contacting Roman provinces

The Compact secretly approaches several Roman provinces, especially former Mandate territories.

It offers:

- trade concessions
- cultural cooperation
- regional autonomy support
- financial links
- political coordination

The Compact hopes these provinces still see Rome as foreign.

Most refuse.

After several generations of:

- common citizenship
- national investment
- shared law
- Latin education
- military service
- access to the civic floor

the former Mandate provinces increasingly regard themselves as Roman.

Some still resent central authority.

Few want separation.

The Compact badly misjudges the depth of Roman naturalisation.

---

## 38. A limited Roman scandal

Rome exposes the Compact’s contacts publicly.

Some politicians demand sanctions or covert retaliation.

The government chooses restraint.

It issues diplomatic protests but avoids escalation.

The Republic argues that provincial loyalty is best demonstrated through free refusal, not political repression.

This response strengthens Roman legitimacy in the targeted provinces.

The Compact becomes more cautious.

---

## 39. Recovery from the crisis

The volcanic aerosols begin clearing after 117,431.

Harvests improve gradually.

Rome’s economy begins recovering around **117,436**.

Full recovery takes more than a decade.

The crisis changes economic policy permanently.

Rome adopts:

- larger food reserves
- stricter limits on speculative lending
- longer project financing horizons
- emergency support for provincial banks
- mandatory climate-risk planning
- wider use of low-energy fallback agriculture

The Republic also reduces the number of grand projects begun simultaneously.

---

## 40. The fate of the Atlantic League after the crisis

The League survives but is diminished.

Several more states leave.

Those that remain accept tighter coordination.

The League becomes smaller, more cautious and more dependent on Roman trade.

It retains:

- maritime expertise
- major ports
- independent finance
- diplomatic prestige

It no longer possesses a credible path to rival Rome globally.

Its main objective becomes survival with dignity.

---

## 41. The asteroid discovery

The asteroid is discovered by Mars in **117,462**.

Martian astronomers detect a dark object approaching from a direction difficult to observe from Terra because it lies close to the Sun in Terran skies.

The object is designated **Maris-117462**.

It is approximately **1.8 kilometres** in diameter.

Its orbit indicates a significant probability of impact with Terra in forty-one years.

Mars transmits the warning through the relay network.

Without Martian observation, Terra would probably not detect the asteroid for another twelve to fifteen years.

That delay could have made effective intervention impossible.

---

## 42. Initial impact probability

Early calculations estimate an impact probability of approximately **38%**.

Later observations raise it above **60%**.

The likely impact energy would be catastrophic regionally and climatically severe globally.

It would not equal Nemesis.

It could still kill tens or hundreds of millions and damage Terra’s recovery for generations.

The asteroid does not appear to contain field-memory material.

The public nevertheless fears another IRF event.

---

## 43. Can Rome stop it?

At current technology, Rome cannot simply destroy the asteroid with one weapon.

It can alter its orbit.

The long warning time makes deflection possible.

Rome chooses a layered strategy:

1. precise observation from Mars and Terra;
2. relay-assisted orbital tracking;
3. multiple kinetic impactors;
4. attached solar sails and reflective coatings;
5. slow mass ejection from the asteroid surface;
6. final emergency fragmentation attempts if deflection fails.

No nuclear devices are available.

Fusion remains impossible.

The plan relies on repeated small changes accumulated over decades.

---

## 44. The Planetary Defence Coalition

Rome invites:

- the Atlantic League
- the Serevan Compact
- independent states
- Mars
- Luna

to participate.

The coalition is coordinated by Rome but legally international.

Mars provides tracking.

Luna provides launch support and deep-space communication.

The League contributes navigation expertise and launch manufacturing.

The Compact contributes observatories and materials.

The project becomes the first major planetary undertaking since the Unification War involving all significant Terran blocs.

---

## 45. The first interception

The first kinetic impactor launches in **117,468**.

It uses advanced propulsion near Terra, then transitions to slow cruise.

The impact succeeds but alters the asteroid’s trajectory less than predicted.

A second impactor fails to reach the target.

A third succeeds.

Over the next twenty years, Rome launches a sequence of missions.

Some:

- strike the asteroid
- attach reflective sheets
- release dust to alter solar heating
- install crude surface engines
- map internal fractures

Others fail completely.

The campaign is expensive and politically controversial.

---

## 46. The low-energy asteroid machinery

The most successful systems use surprisingly modest power.

Once attached to the asteroid, they operate over years.

They include:

- solar-heated mass ejectors
- mechanically timed surface drills
- low-thrust chemical vents
- reflective panels
- slow rotating flywheel systems
- intermittent radio beacons

The asteroid’s orbit changes by tiny amounts.

Across decades, those tiny changes accumulate.

This is exactly the kind of engineering Rome’s low-energy programme advocates.

---

## 47. Successful neutralisation

By **117,489**, revised calculations show that Maris-117462 will miss Terra.

The final safe margin is still small.

A last correction mission launches from Luna.

It adjusts the asteroid’s path enough to eliminate the remaining uncertainty.

The asteroid passes Terra safely in **117,503**, just beyond the end of this century’s main timeframe.

By year 117,500, however, impact has already been ruled out with overwhelming confidence.

Rome has successfully defended Terra from an asteroid.

Mars’s early warning was decisive.

Without it, the remaining preparation time would likely have been insufficient.

---

## 48. The meaning of the defence

The victory transforms Mars–Terra relations.

Mars is publicly credited as the discoverer and first defender.

Roman schools teach that planetary defence began not on Terra, but with a weak signal sent from a world of fewer than half a million people.

The event validates:

- interplanetary communication
- lunar settlement
- the relay network
- low-energy engineering
- international cooperation
- permanent asteroid monitoring

The defence becomes one of Rome’s strongest arguments for continuing its long-term projects even during economic difficulty.

---

## 49. Mars during the century

Mars grows from roughly **220,000–300,000** inhabitants in 117,400 to approximately **300,000–420,000** by 117,500.

Its IRF remains around **81–87%**.

## Society

The Martian Covenant becomes more coordinated but remains a federation of settlements.

## Daily life

Maintenance duties remain universal.

Agriculture is biologically diversified after the earlier crisis.

## Technology

Mars uses:

- geothermal systems
- low-voltage grids
- mechanical computation
- biological life support
- Class 100-compatible communication equipment

## Communication

Permanent Latin dominates interplanetary communication.

Martian local languages continue thriving internally.

## Outlook

Mars is secure enough to grow slowly.

The asteroid discovery gives it renewed purpose as humanity’s outer observatory.

---

## 50. Mars’s role in historical reconstruction

Mars continues contributing records about:

- late lunar civilisation
- refugee movements
- failed rescue attempts
- early IRF expansion
- the contraction of interplanetary society

Martian records sometimes contradict Roman and lunar accounts.

The combined history is more complete and less flattering to every side.

Rome treats this as a strength.

The official curriculum emphasises that catastrophe does not excuse political secrecy or unequal evacuation.

---

## 51. Europa during the century

Europa’s population likely reaches **190,000–380,000**.

Its IRF remains near 100%.

The first Jovian relay probes begin improving communication late in the century.

Data rates remain extremely low.

Rome receives:

- population estimates
- vent maps
- linguistic samples
- ecological records
- fragments of Europan history

Permanent Latin becomes the main external technical language.

Europa remains politically decentralised.

Its Oceanic Assembly grows more regular but still lacks coercive power.

---

## 52. Titan during the century

Titan reaches approximately **20–28 million inhabitants**.

Its IRF remains effectively at 100%.

Titan’s societies continue refining low-energy technologies.

Roman Class 100 research increasingly draws on Titanian experience.

Communication remains weak, but the first Saturn-bound relay probes are en route.

A few Titanian federations formally adopt Permanent Latin as an archival secondary language for future interplanetary exchange.

Titan remains stable, conservative and largely self-contained.

---

## 53. Novaterra during the century

Novaterra’s population rises to approximately **90–130 million**.

Its full-strength IRF prevents industrial development.

Its biosphere continues spreading.

The nine major states remain independent.

The Novaterran Concord gains stronger authority over:

- quarantine
- biosphere corridors
- water systems
- atmospheric management
- ecological emergencies

Daily life remains centred on:

- agriculture
- ecological maintenance
- wind and water power
- mechanical transport
- low-voltage electricity
- public science

The planet’s green regions expand, but enormous barren zones remain.

Novaterra still has no contact with Sol.

---

## 54. The endless colony ship

The fleeing colony ship continues beyond the IRF frontier.

Its astronomers detect more evidence that the shell behind them is thinning unevenly.

They still do not understand the source.

A minority begins arguing that the ship should slow and study the field rather than flee indefinitely.

The majority rejects the proposal.

The ship continues onward.

Its society remains industrial, enclosed and increasingly culturally distinct from planetary humanity.

---

## 55. Recovery of AI knowledge

Lunar archaeology and Terran ruins yield more AI material.

Rome recovers:

- partial training archives
- machine reasoning libraries
- language models
- expert systems
- ethical constraints
- cognitive compression methods
- maintenance records for Dormant Minds

No full ancient AI is awakened.

The available hardware cannot reproduce the original systems.

Roman engineers create large low-energy reasoning engines inspired by ancient AI.

These systems assist with:

- archive search
- legal analysis
- medical diagnosis
- engineering optimisation
- asteroid trajectory calculation
- language preservation

They are not treated as conscious.

The question remains open.

---

## 56. The Low-Energy Intelligence Project

Rome begins researching whether machine intelligence can be implemented in Class 100 systems.

Possible approaches include:

- relay networks
- optical logic
- mechanical neural approximations
- fluidic computation
- distributed slow processing

The systems would be enormous and slow.

A complex answer might require hours or days.

The goal is not to recreate industrial superintelligence.

It is to preserve machine-assisted reasoning even under full IRF intensity.

Blueprints are distributed to every Great Library.

---

## 57. The cultural effect

Long Age engineering and lunar archaeology transform Roman design.

Public machinery becomes ornate and durable.

New rail stations resemble civic monuments.

Waterworks display their mechanisms openly.

Hospitals use standard Permanent Latin labels engraved into replaceable metal plates.

Even emergency pumps are designed to be understood and repaired by future generations.

The aesthetic carries a moral claim:

A machine should explain itself to the people who depend upon it.

---

## 58. Terra by year 117,500

Terra’s population reaches approximately **7.2–7.8 billion**.

The Roman Republic contains roughly **5.8–6.5 billion**.

The population increase reflects:

- long peace
- medical improvement
- low infant mortality
- food security
- continued accession before the crisis
- recovery after the Four Dark Summers

Growth is slowing in wealthy regions.

Poorer associated states retain higher birth rates.

---

## 59. Roman living standards

Rome recovers fully from the financial crisis by the late 117,440s.

By 117,500, most citizens have access to:

- reliable food
- clean water
- public healthcare
- electricity
- education
- transportation
- the civic floor
- legal protection
- communication networks

The universal civic floor remains modest.

It prevents destitution but does not provide a life of luxury.

Regional differences persist, though they are smaller than a century earlier.

---

## 60. The Serevan Compact by year 117,500

The Compact survives.

It does not become a true rival to Rome.

Its members cooperate on:

- defence
- trade
- finance
- diplomacy

But internal differences remain large.

Some members quietly depend on Roman markets.

Others seek closer association.

The Compact’s main success is preserving formal independence during a period of Roman dominance.

Its main failure is creating a fully independent economic system.

---

## 61. The Atlantic League by year 117,500

The League remains intact in reduced form.

It is:

- smaller
- poorer relative to Rome
- more dependent on Roman trade
- less politically ambitious

It still controls important ports and financial institutions.

Its relationship with Rome is cooperative but unequal.

The League’s survival during the financial crisis depends partly on Roman emergency trade.

This ends any realistic prospect of renewed geopolitical confrontation for the foreseeable future.

---

## 62. Rome’s accession policy resumes

After economic recovery, Rome lifts the accession suspension in **117,447**.

Applications are processed slowly.

States that collapsed during the crisis face strict conditions:

- constitutional reform
- anti-corruption oversight
- public referenda
- fiscal transparency
- adoption of Roman standards

Some are admitted.

Others remain associated states.

Rome has become more cautious about absorbing governments simply because they are desperate.

It wants durable consent, not emergency annexation.

---

## 63. The state of the grand programmes

By year 117,500:

## Descending Technology

A large and successful research field, though true Class 100 industrial civilisation remains incomplete.

## Permanent Solar Relay Network

Operational around Terra, Luna and Mars; early outer-system elements are in transit.

## Luna Restituta

Expanded to a permanent population approaching two hundred thousand.

## Lunar archaeology

Producing major historical, biological and technical discoveries.

## Latin preservation

Effective but increasingly strict and socially contentious.

## Great Libraries

Slowed by the crisis but still expanding.

## Monument restoration

Substantially delayed, then partially resumed.

## Biosphere restoration

More cautious, slower and scientifically stronger.

## AI recovery

Rich in theory and fragments, but no full ancient mind restored.

## Planetary defence

Proven capable of deflecting a kilometre-scale asteroid with decades of warning.

---

## 64. The permanent lesson

By the end of the century, Rome’s survival doctrine is expressed in a phrase taught throughout the Republic:

> **Ascend when possible. Descend without dying.**

The Republic no longer assumes technological progress will be continuous.

It expects:

- IRF changes
- asteroid threats
- economic crises
- ecological shocks
- political disruption

Its goal is not to prevent every setback.

It is to ensure that no setback becomes civilisational extinction.

---

## 65. Year 117,500

On Terra, a restored Long Age waterworks supplies a modern city without powered pumps.

In a Great Library, blueprints for Class 100 machinery are stored beside AI source archives recovered from Luna.

On the Moon, Roman archaeologists reconstruct the final days of the civilisation that died there while new residents live behind layered pressure doors labelled only in Permanent Latin.

Around Mars, a primitive relay repeats weak messages between two worlds.

Farther outward, slow probes move toward Jupiter and Saturn, carrying machines designed to speak only a few words at a time—but to keep speaking even if the prison returns at full strength.

The Atlantic League survives by trading with Rome.

The Serevan Compact guards its independence.

Mars watches the dark approaches to Terra.

And somewhere beyond the Solar System, humanity continues both planting worlds and fleeing through the void.

Rome has not made itself immortal.

But it has begun designing a civilisation that knows how to survive losing almost everything.
