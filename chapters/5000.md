# 5000 — The Pace of History

Compare the final 200 years of the pre-Impact baseline:

- Steam
- Railways
- Electricity
- Cars
- Aircraft
- Computers
- Internet
- AI
- Spaceflight

Now imagine instead that, over the same period, the biggest changes were:

- slightly better medicine
- improved crop varieties
- more efficient canals
- a new style of architecture
- a revised legal system

That's roughly what the last **2,000 years** might feel like to them.

---

# Civilisation itself becomes the monument

By now, some institutions are unimaginably old.

Consider:

- a university continuously operating for 3,500 years
- a parliament whose constitution has evolved for two millennia
- a library whose oldest books are 4,000 years old

People would think in historical scales we almost never do.

---

# Cities

The oldest cities become masterpieces.

Every generation improves them slightly.

Instead of demolishing neighbourhoods:

- buildings are restored
- streets adjusted
- canals expanded
- gardens added

A city might resemble a living organism more than a construction project.

---

# Science

Almost all science accessible without industrial instrumentation has been exhausted.

Scholars know:

- biology
- chemistry
- astronomy
- mathematics

to extraordinary levels.

But every frontier now requires instruments they cannot build.

Science becomes increasingly philosophical.

---

# Mathematics flourishes

This may become humanity's greatest intellectual pursuit.

Unlike particle accelerators...

...mathematics needs only minds.

By 5000 they could possess mathematical theories far beyond ours.

Perhaps they've solved problems we still struggle with.

---

# Astronomy

Gigantic observatories cover mountains.

Lenses several metres across are painstakingly polished over decades.

They know:

- thousands of exoplanets
- stellar evolution
- galactic structure

They simply cannot visit any of it.

---

# Daily life

Life is comfortable.

Not luxurious by our standards.

Comfortable by theirs.

A typical person enjoys:

- excellent education
- clean water
- reliable food
- beautiful homes
- strong communities
- abundant culture

Yet they still:

- walk
- cycle
- ride horses
- sail ships

---

# Would electricity become widespread?

This is actually the most interesting question.

## The short answer:

**Probably yes—but not in the way we know it.**

---

### Electricity doesn't require industrialisation to exist.

People could discover:

- electromagnetism
- batteries
- generators

without steam engines.

The problem is **scale**.

---

### What they probably would have

By 5000 they might develop:

### Local power

Individual buildings might have:

- small water turbines
- wind generators
- chemical batteries
- flywheel storage

Enough to power:

- lights
- laboratory equipment
- communication devices

---

### City electricity

Large cities near rivers might build:

- hydroelectric water wheels
- primitive dams
- mechanical-to-electrical generators

Electric street lighting could exist.

Public buildings could have electricity.

---

### Telegraph networks

Almost certainly.

Possibly evolved into astonishing systems.

Potentially:

- teleprinters
- electromechanical messaging
- nationwide communication networks

Something resembling a mechanical internet.

---

### Electric motors?

Probably.

But only where worthwhile.

For example:

- workshops
- cranes
- mills
- elevators

---

### Household electricity?

Possibly.

Although limited.

Perhaps enough for:

- lighting
- clocks
- fans
- pumps

But not:

- refrigerators
- washing machines
- microwave ovens

Those require much more manufacturing sophistication.

---

# Why electricity alone isn't enough

Electricity is only one piece.

Modern civilisation also depends on:

- precision metallurgy
- chemical industries
- plastics
- semiconductor manufacturing
- precision bearings
- mass production

Without those...

Electricity helps enormously...

...but doesn't create computers.

---

# Consider Victorian electricity forever improving

A good analogy is:

Consider electrical technology progressed for 3,000 years...

...while manufacturing remained closer to the late 18th century.

You might get:

- incredibly reliable generators
- elegant electric lighting
- sophisticated relay logic
- electromechanical control systems

But never microchips.

---

# Could they build computers?

Not electronic computers.

But...

Relay computers?

Probably.

Mechanical-electrical hybrids?

Very likely.

Consider something halfway between:

- Charles Babbage's engines
- telephone exchanges
- electromechanical calculators
- railway signalling systems

Scaled up enormously.

Government ministries and universities might own machines capable of millions of operations per day—still vastly slower than a modern laptop, but useful for astronomy, engineering, and administration.

---

# Society by 5000

At this point they are almost unimaginably experienced.

They have accumulated:

- 7,000 years of recorded history
- millennia of continuous scholarship
- centuries of peace punctuated by occasional wars
- enormous cultural continuity

A person from this civilisation might see us as brilliant but impatient.

We would probably see them as technologically conservative.

Both would be partly right.

---

## The biggest irony

By 5000, they may know **exactly** how to build a smartphone.

They could draw every circuit.

Explain every transistor.

Calculate every radio frequency.

They simply could not manufacture ten billion transistors on a fingernail-sized piece of silicon.

In other words, they might possess the *knowledge* of a civilisation beyond ours, while remaining materially constrained by the absence of large-scale industrial production.

So electricity would likely become widespread and important, but it would stop short of triggering the cascade that, in late pre-Impact centuries, led from generators to electronics, computers, and the digital age. It would make this civilisation more efficient and comfortable, but it would not fundamentally change its character in the same way.
