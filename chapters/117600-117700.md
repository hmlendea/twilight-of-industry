# 117600-117700 — The Century of Recovery and Rivalry

## 1. IRF conditions

The background decline continues smoothly.

| Year | Terra average | Wound of Return | Luna | Mars | Europa | Titan |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 117,600 | 55.1% | 44–47% | 61–65% | 78–84% | 96–100% | ~100% |
| 117,650 | 54.6% | 43–46% | 60–64% | 77–83% | 96–100% | ~100% |
| 117,700 | 54.1% | 42–45% | 59–63% | 76–82% | 95–100% | ~100% |

The decline produces modest improvements around Terra and Luna:

- orbital propulsion becomes slightly more reliable;
- low-density electronics become smaller;
- electrical networks tolerate higher peak loads;
- lunar transport costs fall gradually;
- some Martian machinery can operate for longer before instability appears.

Europa and Titan see almost no practical change.

The next scheduled resonance burst remains due in **118,110**.

---

## 2. 117,603 — The Universal Archive Survey

The Roman monument programme had already catalogued physical structures. Its archivists had also been searching technical records for Class100 designs.

In **117,603**, the Senate separates this work into a new institution:

## The Universal Archive Survey

### *Census Archivorum Universalis*

Its mission is to index, study and connect every accessible historical record possessed by the Republic.

This includes:

- Great Library collections;
- provincial archives;
- lunar databases;
- recovered orbital records;
- monastery and temple collections;
- archaeological inscriptions;
- private family records;
- military archives;
- industrial data cores;
- visual and audio media;
- pre-Roman collections purchased abroad.

The programme does not merely preserve documents. It asks what each record refers to, where related material may exist, and how contradictory accounts can be reconciled.

Ancient collections that had survived independently for tens of thousands of years are finally cross-referenced systematically.

---

## 3. 117,606–117,620 — Human history becomes clearer

The Survey produces major discoveries about The First Age.

Previously, historians understood the broad outlines well:

- the ancient Mediterranean civilisations;
- republican and imperial Rome;
- early religious and philosophical traditions;
- the decline and transformation of First Age institutions;
- the first centuries of the Long Age.

But the historical record had been assembled from incomplete copies, later summaries and archaeological interpretation.

The new archive work reveals that some supposedly settled facts were wrong.

## Revised ancient Roman history

Between **117,608 and 117,617**, historians discover:

- several ancient Roman laws previously known only through later paraphrases;
- evidence that some political reforms occurred decades earlier than accepted;
- records of provincial civic participation that had been underestimated;
- correspondence showing that several famous military decisions were driven more by logistics and internal politics than heroic strategy;
- evidence that some ancient “single events” were later combinations of separate episodes;
- clearer records of women’s economic and religious roles;
- previously lost regional forms of Latin;
- more complete descriptions of early republican institutions.

The recovered information neither glorifies nor discredits ancient Rome completely.

It makes it more human, complex and internally divided.

The modern Republic revises school curricula accordingly.

---

## 4. 117,612 — The Connected History Project

The Universal Archive Survey develops a massive chronological system called the **Connected History Project**.

Every known event receives links to:

- contemporary accounts;
- later interpretations;
- archaeological evidence;
- geographic records;
- population data;
- climate records;
- technological context.

Electromechanical reasoning engines assist archivists by identifying matching names, places and phrases across millions of documents.

The machines cannot conduct historical interpretation independently, but they reveal connections no human research team would find unaided.

One discovery links a short Long Age engineering note to a buried hydraulic system found beneath a modern Roman city. The system is excavated and restored successfully in **117,619**.

It still works after minor repairs.

---

## 5. 117,615 — Construction begins on the Lunar Great Library

Rome begins building the first Great Library outside Terra.

The site lies nearly three kilometres beneath the lunar surface, inside a geologically stable ancient industrial excavation.

The **Great Library of Luna** is intended to preserve:

- Roman constitutional records;
- the Permanent Latin standard;
- Class100 blueprints;
- Terran biological archives;
- lunar history;
- Martian, Europan and Titanian records;
- Industrial Age scientific knowledge;
- copies of Terra’s principal cultural works.

The library must remain recoverable even if Luna loses advanced power.

Its design includes:

- isolated pressure cells;
- mechanically operated archive chambers;
- passive thermal stability;
- manually accessible shelves;
- ceramic and metal records;
- low-energy optical storage;
- biological samples in separate vaults;
- independent air and water loops.

Construction is expected to take several centuries.

---

## 6. 117,621 — The first Lunar Library disaster

A survey team discovers that part of the intended foundation lies above a previously unknown ancient void.

The ceiling collapses during excavation.

Thirty-eight workers die and an entire year of construction is lost.

Investigators find that surviving Industrial Age maps had omitted the chamber because it belonged to a classified military complex.

The accident produces fierce criticism:

- some argue Luna is being expanded too quickly;
- others accuse archaeologists of withholding incomplete data;
- lunar residents demand greater local control over construction;
- Roman safety authorities suspend excavation for eighteen months.

The design is revised to require full acoustic and gravitational mapping before any major lunar construction.

---

## 7. 117,625–117,639 — Lunar civilianisation

Luna’s population passes **650,000** in **117,625**.

A growing share consists of:

- families;
- teachers;
- artists;
- merchants;
- ordinary civil servants;
- craftspeople;
- children born on Luna.

Residents increasingly reject being treated as temporary personnel.

Rome responds with the **Lunar Civic Transition Act of 117,629**.

The act begins transforming selected settlements into ordinary civil jurisdictions.

It creates:

- elected municipal governments;
- civilian courts;
- ordinary property law;
- local taxation;
- family housing;
- public schools;
- civilian hospitals;
- labour protections.

Scientific and archaeological zones remain under special administration.

---

## 8. 117,636 — The first Lunar provinces

On **1 January 117,636**, three large lunar territories become fully fledged Roman provinces:

- Provincia Tranquillitatis
- Provincia Imbrium
- Provincia Serenitatis

Their inhabitants receive the same constitutional rights as citizens in Terran provinces.

They elect:

- provincial assemblies;
- representatives to the National Assembly;
- senators;
- local judges.

The provinces remain subject to exceptional environmental laws, but they are no longer governed as expeditionary territories.

This is the first time since the Closing Sky that Luna contains ordinary constitutional provinces.

---

## 9. Freedom of movement to Luna

Formal freedom of movement is extended to the new provinces.

In practice, movement is limited by transportation capacity.

Rome establishes a separate civilian transport system rather than relying on scientific and archaeological spacecraft.

The **Lunar Civil Passage Service** begins operations in **117,638**.

Seats are allocated through:

- open purchase;
- residency applications;
- family reunification;
- employment placement;
- public-service lotteries.

Critics argue that this is not genuine freedom because:

- fares are expensive;
- the state controls spacecraft;
- health screening remains mandatory;
- annual passenger capacity is limited;
- Luna can suspend arrivals during ecological stress.

The Constitutional Court rules in **117,642** that freedom of movement means equal legal eligibility, not unlimited physical transport.

Rome retains health, training and capacity requirements.

---

## 10. 117,640–117,660 — Lunar expansion

Lunar population grows:

| Year | Population |
| --- | --- |
| 117,600 | 420,000–550,000 |
| 117,625 | ~650,000 |
| 117,650 | ~1.1 million |
| 117,675 | ~1.8 million |
| 117,700 | 2.5–3.1 million |

The increase is fast by Lunar standards but tightly managed.

Ordinary life becomes increasingly normal:

- local elections;
- marriages and family reunions;
- provincial sports competitions;
- theatres;
- permanent markets;
- universities;
- religious and cultural institutions.

Yet every resident still receives emergency training.

Luna remains a world where a civic celebration and an evacuation drill may occur in the same week.

---

## 11. The Lunar Great Library remains unfinished

The library project resumes in **117,623** and advances slowly.

Further setbacks occur:

- 117,647: water contamination forces closure of a construction district;
- 117,661: an archive-lift cable fails, killing nine workers;
- 117,684: a low-frequency structural resonance damages two completed chambers;
- 117,695: funding is redirected temporarily after a lunar food shortage.

By 117,700:

- the main access shaft is complete;
- two emergency archive chambers are operational;
- several Class100 blueprint collections have been deposited;
- less than 8% of the planned structure exists.

The Great Library remains in its earliest phase.

---

## 12. 117,608–117,635 — The Serevan manufacturing boom

The Serevan Compact adopts a radically different economic model from Rome.

Roman regulation requires:

- high durability;
- repairability;
- safety testing;
- long service life;
- design archiving;
- resource continuity.

Serevan manufacturers face much weaker standards.

They produce:

- inexpensive household devices;
- low-cost clothing;
- short-lived machinery;
- simple vehicles;
- mass-produced tools;
- decorative consumer goods.

These products are cheaper, lighter and frequently more fashionable than Roman equivalents.

They are also:

- difficult to repair;
- less reliable;
- resource-intensive over time;
- often designed for replacement rather than maintenance.

Serevan exports flood Roman markets.

---

## 13. Why Roman consumers buy Serevan goods

Many Roman citizens resent being told every household object must last fifty years.

Serevan products offer:

- lower prices;
- rapid design changes;
- greater variety;
- fewer regulatory delays;
- fashionable new forms.

Young consumers especially embrace them.

The phrase **“Serevan bright”** becomes associated with colourful, inexpensive and temporary goods.

Roman durability advocates describe them as **“future ruins.”**

---

## 14. Roman industrial decline

By **117,628**, several Roman consumer industries are in crisis.

Domestic firms struggle because:

- their products cost more;
- approval takes longer;
- repairs reduce repeat sales;
- Serevan firms release new models faster;
- consumers do not always value century-long durability.

Employment falls in Roman manufacturing regions.

Industrial federations demand:

- tariffs;
- equivalent safety standards for imports;
- government procurement preferences;
- restrictions on disposable products.

Free-market parties oppose protection.

They argue that Roman manufacturers should adapt rather than demand shelter.

---

## 15. 117,632 — Atlantic markets turn toward Sereva

The Atlantic League had traditionally imported mostly Roman goods.

As its economy stagnates, price becomes more important than longevity.

By **117,632**, the League imports more manufactured goods from the Serevan Compact than from Rome.

This strengthens the Compact dramatically.

Serevan factories expand faster than domestic demand.

New industrial cities grow along rail and maritime corridors.

The Compact’s population experiences rising wages and rapid urbanisation.

Its leaders begin describing it as a third global economic model:

- Roman durability;
- Atlantic finance;
- Serevan affordability.

---

## 16. 117,635–117,643 — Rome intervenes

Rome initially tries to preserve free trade.

It introduces product labelling showing:

- expected service life;
- repairability;
- Class rating;
- material cost;
- recyclability.

This does not solve the problem.

In **117,639**, the Senate passes the **Equal Standards Trade Act**.

Imported goods must meet minimum Roman civilian safety standards.

They do not need to meet the full durability requirements imposed on Roman public systems, but they must provide:

- truthful lifespan estimates;
- safe materials;
- repair information;
- recycling provisions;
- basic reliability guarantees.

Many Serevan products fail certification.

Imports fall sharply.

---

## 17. The Serevan downturn

The Compact had expanded production on the assumption that Roman and Atlantic demand would continue rising.

When Roman access contracts, factories accumulate unsold goods.

The Atlantic League cannot absorb the surplus.

By **117,645**:

- Serevan warehouses are full;
- prices collapse;
- factories close;
- unemployment rises;
- provincial governments accumulate debt;
- political support for confrontation increases.

Serevan leaders accuse Rome of disguised protectionism.

Rome replies that it is merely applying the same safety principles to imported goods as to domestic products.

---

## 18. 117,647–117,655 — Industrial sabotage

A series of accidents strikes Roman manufacturing.

They include:

- contaminated lubricants;
- corrupted design files;
- warehouse fires;
- defective imported components;
- railway signal failures;
- industrial espionage;
- theft of certification data.

Roman investigators trace several operations to networks receiving money through Serevan commercial institutions.

The Compact denies state involvement.

It argues that smugglers, private companies and displaced workers acted independently.

Rome does not believe this explanation.

---

## 19. The sabotage scandal

The most serious incident occurs on **18 April 117,653**.

A defective pressure component causes an explosion in a Roman pharmaceutical plant.

More than 1,700 people die.

Production of several important medicines is disrupted.

Investigators discover that the component had been deliberately substituted during transport.

The replacement carried forged Roman certification marks.

Evidence links the operation to a Serevan state-owned trading company.

The Compact claims the evidence was fabricated.

Roman public opinion turns sharply hostile.

---

## 20. 117,655 — The ultimatum

On **2 September 117,655**, Rome delivers the **Ultimatum of Ravenna Nova**.

The Serevan Compact must:

1. permit joint investigation of sabotage networks;
2. arrest named suspects;
3. dismantle state-connected covert industrial units;
4. compensate victims;
5. accept international monitoring of specified trade institutions;
6. end forged Roman certification.

If the Compact refuses, Rome will impose:

- a total trade embargo;
- suspension of all Roman investment;
- exclusion from Roman payment systems;
- denial of Roman technical licences;
- suspension of infrastructure cooperation.

Rome gives the Compact ninety days.

---

## 21. The Compact’s response

Initially, Serevan leaders reject the ultimatum.

They denounce it as an attempt to impose Roman jurisdiction on sovereign states.

Several Compact members begin preparing for embargo.

But internal divisions quickly emerge.

Industrial states favour resistance.

Agricultural members fear losing Roman machinery and medicine.

Smaller states worry the Compact will sacrifice them to protect covert operations conducted by larger members.

The Atlantic League refuses to support the Compact diplomatically.

It cannot risk Roman retaliation.

---

## 22. 117,656 — The Compact backs down

Two weeks before the deadline, five Serevan governments threaten to suspend their participation in the Compact unless a settlement is reached.

On **19 November 117,655**, the Compact accepts a modified agreement.

It does not admit official responsibility.

It agrees to:

- a mixed investigatory commission;
- prosecution of individuals;
- closure of several trading agencies;
- compensation through a commercial fund;
- verification of export certification;
- a five-year ban on specified firms.

Rome does not impose the full embargo.

It imposes targeted restrictions.

The immediate crisis ends without war.

Relations remain extremely poor.

---

## 23. Political consequences inside Rome

The Serevan confrontation produces two major Roman movements.

## The Sovereign Market movement

It argues that Rome must protect domestic industry and national safety standards from foreign undercutting.

## The World Union movement

It argues that recurring crises prove Terra cannot remain divided.

Its supporters demand:

- eventual annexation or accession of all remaining states;
- one world government;
- uniform product standards;
- one planetary legal order.

The World Union movement attracts:

- military planners;
- major industrial figures;
- some provincial politicians;
- planetary-defence advocates.

It does not advocate immediate war officially.

Some members openly argue that peaceful unification should be backed by overwhelming force.

The movement will remain influential for centuries.

---

## 24. Roman critics of the ultimatum

Pacifists and commercial liberals accuse the Republic of overreaction.

They argue that:

- evidence of central Serevan control was incomplete;
- the ultimatum resembled economic coercion;
- total embargo would have harmed ordinary people;
- Rome increasingly behaves as though its laws apply everywhere.

The government replies that sabotage causing mass casualties is not ordinary competition.

The Senate commissions an independent review.

The review concludes that high-level Serevan involvement was likely but not fully proven.

This ambiguity prevents full reconciliation.

---

## 25. Roman territorial expansion

Rome continues growing through accession.

Between **117,600 and 117,700**, five sovereign states join the Republic.

Three are poor associated states seeking:

- fiscal stability;
- healthcare;
- infrastructure;
- food security.

Two are former Atlantic League states that had remained independent for more than a century.

Their accessions are peaceful and approved by strong referenda.

By 117,700, Terra contains **16 sovereign states**:

| Political grouping | Number of states | Population share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Roman Republic | 1 | 89–91% |
| Serevan Compact | 7 | 5–6% |
| Atlantic League | 3 | 1.5–2.5% |
| Other independent states | 5 | 2–3% |

The Roman Republic governs approximately:

- 9.0–9.6 billion people;
- around 77% of inhabited land;
- more than 90% of Terra’s economic output.

Terra’s total population reaches approximately **10.0–10.6 billion**.

---

## 26. The Atlantic League

The League loses another member in **117,622** and a second in **117,668**.

It is now reduced to three sovereign states and several jointly administered ports.

Its economy is increasingly tied to Serevan goods and Roman finance.

The League remains important in:

- shipping;
- insurance;
- maritime law;
- port engineering.

It is no longer a major political counterweight.

Talks with the Serevan Compact continue, but full merger remains impossible.

The League fears becoming economically subordinate to Serevan industry just as it had once feared Roman domination.

---

## 27. Mars begins resettling abandoned settlements

Mars launches the **Return to the Silent Settlements Programme** in **117,607**.

Its goals are to:

- recover abandoned infrastructure;
- expand population capacity;
- retrieve archives;
- restore geothermal systems;
- reconnect isolated regions.

The first target is **Ares Vallis Three**, abandoned more than seven thousand years earlier after water-system failure.

Roman Class100 designs and lower IRF intensity make restoration conceivable.

---

## 28. 117,611–117,619 — Ares Vallis Three

The first teams discover that the settlement is not simply dead.

Its lower chambers contain:

- intact water reserves;
- dormant microbial ecosystems;
- sealed archives;
- structurally stable housing;
- toxic fungal growth;
- unstable pressure zones.

The restoration takes eight years.

The most dangerous problem is biological.

Ancient microorganisms have evolved in isolation and react unpredictably with modern Martian crops.

A quarantine failure in **117,616** sickens 340 workers and kills twelve.

The settlement is reopened permanently in **117,619** with a population of 3,000.

By 117,700, it supports nearly 18,000 people.

---

## 29. The failure at Elysium Deep

Mars’s second major attempt is less successful.

**Elysium Deep** contains an excellent geothermal source and large caverns.

In **117,628**, restoration teams reactivate ancient heat channels.

The change destabilises frozen ground above the settlement.

A large subsurface collapse destroys two completed districts.

More than 1,100 people die.

The settlement is abandoned again.

The disaster shows that old infrastructure cannot always be trusted simply because it once worked.

Mars creates much stricter geological review rules.

Elysium Deep becomes a memorial and research site.

---

## 30. Martian long-term expansion

Despite setbacks, Mars continues reopening settlements.

By **117,700**:

- six abandoned settlements have been permanently resettled;
- four remain under reconstruction;
- three restoration attempts have been abandoned;
- dozens are still being surveyed.

Martian population rises from roughly **500,000** in 117,600 to **720,000–900,000** in 117,700.

The programme will continue for centuries.

Mars does not expand outward in a simple frontier.

It advances through careful restoration, retreat and revision.

---

## 31. Martian IRF and technology

At approximately **76–82% IRF intensity** by 117,700, Mars gains modest capabilities:

- more reliable medium-power generators;
- stronger radio transmitters;
- somewhat faster surface vehicles;
- improved chemical manufacturing;
- larger electromechanical computers.

High-energy industry remains impossible.

Martian engineers use the weakening primarily to increase redundancy rather than consumption.

They remain far more cautious than many Terran independent states.

---

## 32. Novaterra grows new countries

Novaterra’s biosphere continues spreading into newly habitable regions.

As mature ecological corridors reach distant valleys and coasts, settlements become less dependent on the nine original states.

New political communities emerge.

Between **117,620 and 117,695**, five new internationally recognised countries form.

Three develop peacefully from autonomous frontier regions.

One emerges after a constitutional separation.

One forms from several ecological cooperatives that merge to manage a new forest-and-river system.

By 117,700, Novaterra contains:

- 14 major sovereign states;
- dozens of autonomous ecological territories;
- the Novaterran Concord.

The Concord remains responsible for planetary biosphere rules but not ordinary government.

---

## 33. Novaterra’s population and ecology

Novaterran population reaches approximately **180–240 million** by 117,700.

The planet is still far from fully green.

Approximately:

- 22–28% of land supports mature ecosystems;
- another 20% supports developing microbial, grassland or wetland systems;
- the rest remains barren or only lightly colonised.

The new countries emerge mainly where ecosystems have become productive enough to sustain independent populations.

This creates tension: political borders increasingly follow ecological maturity rather than geography alone.

---

## 34. Old orbital habitats

Several Industrial Age orbital habitats still remain in high or stable orbits.

Most are severely damaged.

Rome selects three for possible restoration:

- Habitat Aurelia
- Habitat Kepler
- Habitat Mnemosyne

Each has a different planned purpose.

---

## 35. Habitat Aurelia

Aurelia occupies the most convenient orbit but has extensive structural damage.

Rome plans to restore it as:

- a scientific station;
- an asteroid-observation outpost;
- a communications platform.

During the first crewed inspection in **117,614**, astronauts discover that some sealed internal sectors remain pressurised.

Inside are preserved vegetation, human remains and functioning low-power mechanical systems.

The pressure differential makes entry dangerous.

A rushed attempt to open one section causes explosive decompression.

Four astronauts die.

Restoration is suspended for three years.

---

## 36. The Aurelia reversal

When work resumes, engineers discover that the habitat’s main rotational bearing is less damaged than expected.

Aurelia can be stabilised without complete reconstruction.

By **117,633**, it supports a rotating crew of 90.

By **117,660**, it becomes a permanently occupied research outpost with roughly 600 residents.

It later becomes a key early-warning station for asteroids approaching from difficult solar angles.

---

## 37. Habitat Kepler

Kepler is chosen as a communications station.

Its orbit provides useful geometry for signals toward Mars and the outer Solar System.

The structure initially appears stable.

In **117,641**, crews find evidence that the habitat had been deliberately damaged during the final orbital conflicts of the Dark Age.

Several compartments contain unexploded weapons.

One detonates during removal, killing twenty-seven people and destroying the planned relay section.

Rome considers abandoning the project.

Instead, the surviving framework is converted into a smaller unmanned relay platform.

Kepler never becomes a settlement.

It becomes a warning about the long persistence of warfare’s debris.

---

## 38. Habitat Mnemosyne

Mnemosyne occupies the most stable orbit of the three.

It is smaller than Aurelia and difficult to reach.

Rome chooses it for the **Orbital Knowledge Vault**.

Unlike a Great Library, it is not publicly accessible.

Its purpose is to preserve a compact but comprehensive recovery archive.

It contains:

- Permanent Latin references;
- constitutional documents;
- Class100 designs;
- medical summaries;
- agricultural information;
- astronomical tables;
- biological data;
- compressed cultural works;
- instructions for rebuilding basic industry.

Media include:

- engraved metal;
- ceramic plates;
- microphotographic film;
- magnetic storage;
- optical crystals.

---

## 39. The Mnemosyne Vault

Restoration begins in **117,648**.

The habitat’s stable orbit reduces collision risk but does not eliminate it.

In **117,657**, a previously untracked fragment penetrates an outer compartment.

No crew is present.

The impact destroys a year of stored material.

Rome revises the vault into multiple isolated cells rather than one central archive.

By **117,682**, the first permanent archive cells are sealed.

A rotating team of twelve to twenty archivists visits periodically.

There is no permanent population.

Mnemosyne becomes a literal trove of knowledge above Terra.

---

## 40. 117,671 — The Great Solar Flare

On **7 June 117,671**, Sol produces the largest recorded solar flare of the modern Dark Age.

The associated coronal mass ejection reaches Terra two days later.

The flare is not caused by the IRF, though the field complicates its effects.

It produces:

- intense geomagnetic storms;
- electrical surges;
- radio blackouts;
- satellite failures;
- auroras visible at unusually low latitudes;
- disruption of long conductors;
- erratic behaviour in high-output machines.

---

## 41. Effects on Terra

Roman systems are better protected than Industrial Age infrastructure would have been because they are decentralised and designed for fallback operation.

Even so:

- regional grids fail;
- rail signalling is disrupted;
- long-distance radio becomes unusable;
- several orbital tracking systems go blind;
- electrical fires break out;
- hospitals switch to low-energy emergency modes.

The worst civilian disaster occurs when a provincial reservoir-control network receives corrupted signals.

Floodgates open incorrectly.

Several downstream towns are flooded.

More than 18,000 people die.

The event leads to mandatory mechanical local overrides for all major water systems.

---

## 42. Effects on Luna

Luna lacks atmospheric protection.

Radiation levels rise sharply.

Residents shelter underground.

Several surface construction crews cannot return in time.

Thirty-one workers die from acute exposure.

The Lunar Civil Passage Service suspends flights for six weeks.

The solar event also damages parts of the communications network.

Luna’s strict compartmentalisation prevents broader disaster.

The accident strengthens support for deeper residential districts and better solar-warning systems.

---

## 43. Effects on orbital infrastructure

Concordia Orbitalis enters emergency shutdown.

Its crews shelter behind water and material shielding.

Two external modules lose power.

A Serevan-built experimental component fails much earlier than its certified lifespan, intensifying the trade dispute.

Habitat Aurelia loses communications for eleven days.

The Mnemosyne Vault remains intact because most archive systems are passive.

The event proves the value of maintaining records that require no power.

---

## 44. Effects on Mars

Mars receives warning from Terra’s solar observatories before the main particle event arrives.

The Martian Covenant orders shelter protocols.

Surface teams retreat underground.

Several old settlements lose communications.

One recently resettled outpost, **Noctis Minor**, suffers a complete radio failure and is presumed lost.

After three months, a ground expedition finds the settlement alive.

Its Class100 mechanical systems had continued operating even after every advanced electronic component failed.

The survival becomes one of the strongest arguments for the Human Continuity Exchange.

---

## 45. Effects on Europa and Titan

Europa is largely shielded beneath ice.

Surface communication installations are damaged, causing years of reduced contact.

Titan’s dense atmosphere and underground civilisation provide excellent protection.

The principal effect there is communication loss.

The flare reaches the Saturnian relay network as electrical disturbance, corrupting several years of accumulated outbound historical data.

Titanian archivists resend it slowly.

---

## 46. The Solar Resilience Reforms

After the flare, Rome mandates:

- passive solar-storm warnings;
- mechanical grid isolation;
- local control of water and transport systems;
- shielded emergency communication;
- deeper lunar shelters;
- radiation-hardened orbital modules;
- non-electrical archive copies;
- independent clocks and navigation tables.

The flare temporarily vindicates Rome’s supposedly paranoid regulation.

Even critics admit that lower standards would have produced far worse losses.

---

## 47. Species restoration successes

The Great Terran Restoration achieves several important breakthroughs.

## 117,618 — Restored large herbivores

A species that had repeatedly failed due to digestive disease is successfully integrated into a protected grassland ecosystem after scientists reconstruct missing gut microbes from lunar records.

The population becomes self-sustaining by **117,642**.

## 117,651 — Reef restoration

A reconstructed reef-building organism survives in open marine conditions after earlier generations failed.

By 117,690, small living reef systems exist in three protected seas.

## 117,674 — Predator reintroduction

A restored predator species is released into a large managed wilderness.

Earlier attempts failed because prey populations were unstable.

This time, extensive ecological modelling and gradual release succeed.

The predator begins reproducing naturally.

---

## 48. Rome’s refusal to abandon species

The programme maintains genetic lines even after repeated failure.

Some species undergo dozens of unsuccessful trials.

Critics accuse the Republic of wasting resources on organisms that no longer belong in modern ecosystems.

Restorationists reply that failure in one century does not prove impossibility forever.

Rome preserves every viable line unless there is a clear safety threat.

The policy becomes another expression of Perpetuitas: no loss is treated as final while recovery remains conceivable.

---

## 49. The buried-city movement

The pyramid discovery and Universal Archive Survey inspire intensified searches for buried cities.

Between **117,630 and 117,700**, Roman archaeologists identify more than two hundred major buried urban sites.

Only twelve are opened extensively.

Three become active resettlement projects.

One contains layers from The First Age, the Long Age and the Industrial Age built over one another.

The resettled district combines:

- restored ancient streets;
- Long Age water systems;
- Industrial Age transit tunnels;
- modern Roman housing.

It becomes a living demonstration of the four ages.

---

## 50. Terra’s living standards and cohesion

By **117,700**, Roman living standards are high but not perfectly equal.

Most citizens have:

- universal healthcare;
- civic income support;
- durable housing;
- reliable electricity;
- public transport;
- advanced education;
- legal protection;
- emergency reserves.

The Serevan trade crisis produces some unemployment in manufacturing regions, but Roman industrial support prevents mass collapse.

Social cohesion remains strong.

The World Union movement grows, but it does not dominate national elections.

Most Romans prefer gradual peaceful accession to coercive unification.

---

## 51. Human physiology by 117,700

The century produces little major biological change, but some trends deepen.

## Terrans

Remain the least specialised and most biologically diverse.

## Lunars

Become more recognisably low-gravity adapted, though exercise and medicine preserve Terran compatibility.

## Martians

Continue diverging through low-gravity and low-oxygen adaptation.

## Europans

Remain the most aquatic and dim-light adapted.

## Titanians

Remain compact, cold-adapted and metabolically efficient.

## Novaterrans

Remain outwardly Terran-like but immunologically vulnerable to Terra’s complex biosphere.

No population has become a separate species.

Medical experts warn that uncontrolled biological contact between worlds could nevertheless be disastrous.

## 52. Mars in 117,700

- Population: 720,000–900,000
- IRF: 76–82%
- Settlements: roughly 50 major inhabited complexes
- Outlook: cautiously positive

Mars is growing through resettlement rather than new planetary engineering.

Its greatest risk is overextension.

The Covenant therefore imposes strict limits: no restored settlement may rely on one heat source, one crop system or one communication route.

## 53. Luna in 117,700

- Population: 2.5–3.1 million
- IRF: 59–63%
- Roman provinces: three full provinces, several special territories
- Great Library: under 10% structurally complete
- Outlook: growing, but still dependent on Terra

Luna now contains ordinary Roman civilian society.

Its residents increasingly identify as both Lunar and Roman.

Free movement exists legally but remains constrained physically.

## 54. Europa in 117,700

- Population: 260,000–470,000
- IRF: 95–100%
- Political system: Oceanic Assembly plus autonomous communities
- Outlook: stable but geologically vulnerable

The solar flare temporarily disrupts external contact.

Class100 exchange continues through slow relays.

Europa remains the hardest inhabited world to communicate with.

## 55. Titan in 117,700

- Population: 27–36 million
- IRF: ~100%
- Political system: cavern republics and thermal federations
- Outlook: highly stable

Titan continues contributing low-energy technology.

Its societies view the Roman–Serevan economic confrontation as evidence that high-output economies create unnecessary instability.

---

## 56. Novaterra in 117,700

- Population: 180–240 million
- IRF: 100%
- Sovereign states: 14 major states
- Mature green land: 22–28%
- Outlook: steady expansion of its human-made biosphere

Novaterra’s new states intensify political diversity.

The Concord grows stronger ecologically but remains weak politically.

Novaterrans still have no knowledge of current events around Sol.

## 57. The fleeing colony ship

The ship remains beyond the active IRF frontier.

Its scientists now know that the field contains:

- thinning zones;
- waves;
- discontinuities;
- large-scale geometric structure.

They still do not know why.

A political movement called the **Return Inquiry** forms, demanding that probes be sent back toward the field.

The ship’s government authorises unmanned observation craft but refuses to alter its own course.

The first probes begin their long return journey.

## 58. State of the Roman grand projects

By 117,700:

## Universal Archive Survey

Has clarified large parts of The First Age.

## Lunar Great Library

Operational only in a few small archive cells; centuries from completion.

## Monument restoration

Expanded through newly discovered buried cities.

## Biosphere restoration

Achieves several major long-term species successes.

## Orbital recovery

Aurelia restored, Kepler reduced to a relay, Mnemosyne converted into a vault.

## Class100 programme

Strengthened by Martian expansion and the solar flare.

## Planetary unity

Still gradual and voluntary, despite rising demands for immediate world government.

## 59. The political map of Terra, 117,700

Terra contains **16 sovereign states**.

The Roman Republic is overwhelmingly dominant but not universal.

The Serevan Compact remains economically important but politically weakened by the sabotage crisis.

The Atlantic League survives as a small maritime bloc.

Independent states increasingly operate within Roman technical, financial and legal gravity even when they reject accession.

---

## 60. End of the century

The century ends with a contradiction.

Rome has never been stronger.

It controls most of Terra, governs Luna’s first modern provinces, operates restored orbital habitats and possesses the most complete historical archive humanity has ever assembled.

Yet it increasingly defines strength not as the capacity to dominate, but as the capacity to continue functioning after power disappears.

Its buildings are slower to approve.

Its machines are more expensive.

Its laws are accused of paranoia.

Its lunar construction proceeds painfully.

Its species restoration takes generations.

Its archives may take millennia to complete.

Rome accepts this.

It has learned that speed created the Industrial Age, but durability must carry humanity through the Dark Age.

And as the Republic unearths forgotten cities, restores vanished species and rebuilds dead habitats above Terra, its long project becomes clearer:

not merely to recover everything humanity once possessed, but to ensure that nothing recovered can ever again be lost so completely.
