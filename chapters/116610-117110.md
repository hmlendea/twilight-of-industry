# 116610-117110 — The Five Centuries Before the Unification War


## 116610

Terra contains slightly more than **3 billion people**.

Its population is growing, but development is highly uneven.

The most industrialised regions possess:

- continental railways
- reliable regional electricity
- advanced steel production
- chemical industries
- antibiotics and vaccination
- electromechanical data networks
- powered agricultural machinery
- large cities
- organised national armies
- experimental high-altitude vehicles

Other regions still depend primarily on:

- animal labour
- rainfall agriculture
- local workshops
- sailing vessels
- village-scale power
- hand irrigation
- traditional medicine supplemented by limited modern knowledge

This inequality becomes the defining political fact of the next five centuries.

The richer regions need raw materials, labour and food.

The poorer regions need machinery, medicine, investment and access to more stable environments.

Neither side believes the other is offering a fair exchange.

---

## 1. The Roman Republic continues growing

By year 116,610, the Roman Republic dominates much of the Mediterranean world and maintains influence far beyond its formal borders.

Over the next five centuries, it expands through a combination of:

- negotiated accession
- economic dependency
- military intervention
- infrastructure agreements
- citizenship treaties
- defensive alliances
- direct conquest

Rome does not yet unify Terra.

But it becomes the largest single political system on the planet.

By year 117,110, the Republic directly governs or closely associates territories across:

- most of southern and western Europe
- large parts of central Europe
- much of North Africa
- sections of the Nile basin
- the eastern Mediterranean
- parts of western Asia
- several Atlantic islands
- numerous overseas commercial enclaves

Its population approaches **700–900 million**, depending on how associated states are counted.

---

## 2. The mature Roman political system

The Republic remains recognisably republican, but it is no longer the compact federation founded centuries earlier.

Its institutions have multiplied.

The Senate represents provinces, cities and chartered institutions.

The Popular Assembly represents citizens by population.

The Civic Council investigates corruption and abuse.

The Tribunal of Generations reviews long-term risk.

The Consuls remain the formal heads of government.

But real power is increasingly distributed among:

- provincial governors
- industrial ministries
- military commands
- infrastructure authorities
- scientific colleges
- banking federations
- electoral coalitions

Rome remains more democratic than ancient Rome ever was.

It is also becoming more bureaucratic, more militarised and more difficult to reform.

---

## 3. Latin Nova becomes global

Latin Nova continues spreading.

Inside the Republic, nearly every educated citizen speaks it.

Outside Roman territory, it becomes common in:

- diplomacy
- engineering
- international law
- science
- trade
- military communication
- archive studies

Many non-Roman powers dislike this.

They regard the language as a vehicle for Roman influence.

Several rival states promote their own scientific languages or multilingual systems.

Nevertheless, Latin Nova becomes the closest thing Terra has to a shared planetary language.

This gives Rome enormous cultural power even where it lacks political authority.

---

## 4. The Eastern Mandate

Rome’s greatest rival remains the Eastern Mandate.

The Mandate has grown into a vast continental system with hundreds of millions of inhabitants.

Its government is hierarchical and centralised.

It emphasises:

- state planning
- technical education
- industrial discipline
- population management
- centralised food reserves
- long-term infrastructure
- military readiness

The Mandate views Rome’s electoral politics as wasteful and unstable.

Rome views the Mandate as authoritarian and expansionist.

Both claim to represent the most realistic path to planetary unity.

Both believe the other will eventually have to yield.

Neither intends to yield.

---

## 5. The Atlantic League

The Atlantic League remains a network of maritime and commercial powers.

It controls many of Terra’s most important ocean routes.

Its strength comes from:

- shipping
- finance
- colonial ports
- insurance systems
- telecommunications
- industrial manufacturing
- access to mineral-rich territories

The League presents itself as an alternative to both Roman federation and Mandate centralisation.

Its ideology emphasises:

- trade
- private enterprise
- city autonomy
- contractual law
- open seas
- technological innovation

In practice, its wealth depends heavily on unequal relations with poorer regions.

This becomes a major source of resentment.

---

## 6. The Pacific Cavity states

The states surrounding the Wound of Return remain technologically exceptional.

Because the local IRF is weaker than Terra’s average, they can operate machinery that fails elsewhere.

Their laboratories develop:

- more powerful engines
- higher-pressure systems
- improved radio transmitters
- advanced metallurgy
- experimental rockets
- compact electromechanical processors
- stronger industrial furnaces

These states do not form one unified country.

They form a dense, competitive cluster of republics, research cities and industrial federations.

They cooperate to protect the impact region.

They compete fiercely over its technological advantages.

Rome wants privileged access.

The Eastern Mandate wants permanent research bases.

The Atlantic League wants open commercial rights.

The Cavity states want all three powers kept at a distance.

---

## 7. The unequal industrial transition

Between years 116,610 and 116,800, Terra’s wealth gap widens sharply.

Industrial regions accumulate advantages:

- greater crop yields
- better medicine
- lower infant mortality
- stronger armies
- faster communication
- cheaper manufactured goods
- better disaster response

Poor regions face a vicious cycle.

They lack capital to build industry.

Without industry, they cannot compete economically.

Their skilled workers leave.

Their governments borrow from richer powers.

Debt payments force resource exports.

Local environments are damaged by intensive mining or plantation agriculture.

The profits rarely remain local.

This produces a new global division between the **Machine Regions** and the **Labour Regions**.

The names are imprecise but politically powerful.

---

## 8. Environmental deterioration in poorer regions

Climate on Terra is globally stable, but local environments deteriorate under population pressure and unequal development.

In many less-industrialised regions:

- forests are cleared for fuel and export crops
- soils become exhausted
- rivers are diverted for distant industrial cities
- mines contaminate groundwater
- coastal fisheries collapse
- droughts become more destructive
- irrigation systems decay
- wealthier states acquire the best agricultural land

The problem is not a new planetary climate catastrophe.

It is a patchwork of regional ecological crises.

Each crisis appears manageable in isolation.

Together, they displace tens of millions.

---

## 9. The first great migration waves

Around year **116,730**, large population movements begin.

At first they are seasonal.

Workers travel to richer industrial regions for:

- construction
- mining
- factory labour
- harvest work
- military service

Then migration becomes permanent.

Families cross borders.

Villages empty.

Frontier cities expand rapidly.

Rich regions initially welcome the labour.

Factories need workers.

Railways need builders.

Armies need recruits.

Agricultural estates need hands.

But infrastructure does not expand fast enough.

Large migrant districts grow around industrial cities.

They suffer from:

- overcrowding
- poor sanitation
- political exclusion
- dangerous employment
- language barriers
- low wages
- hostility from local populations

---

## 10. Migration becomes political

By year 116,800, migration is no longer treated merely as an economic issue.

It becomes central to politics.

In rich states, anti-migration movements argue that newcomers are:

- depressing wages
- overloading public services
- threatening local cultures
- increasing crime
- carrying disease
- serving foreign political interests

Industrial employers argue that migration is essential.

Humanitarian groups demand equal citizenship.

Military planners see migrants as potential recruits or potential insurgents.

In poor regions, leaders accuse wealthy powers of stealing their young and educated populations.

Some governments attempt to stop emigration.

Others deliberately encourage it to reduce domestic pressure.

---

## 11. The march states

Several wealthy powers create fortified border regions known as **march states**.

These are intended to absorb or block migration.

They receive:

- military funding
- railway construction
- settlement subsidies
- weapons
- food reserves
- political autonomy

In return, they guard the approaches to richer cores.

Over time, many march states become unstable.

They contain:

- local populations
- military settlers
- migrants
- refugees
- criminal networks
- competing foreign agents

Some become semi-permanent war zones.

Others develop hybrid societies and grow into important powers of their own.

---

## 12. The first migrant invasions

The distinction between migration and invasion begins to collapse during the crises of the late 116,800s.

In several regions, large displaced populations arrive at fortified borders and are refused entry.

They do not turn back.

Some are accompanied by local militias.

Others are protected by desperate governments seeking access to richer land.

Rail depots, food stores and border towns are seized.

Military forces respond.

The first major incidents involve tens of thousands.

Later ones involve hundreds of thousands.

These movements are not always centrally organised.

They often consist of:

- refugees
- deserters
- armed farmers
- local militias
- religious communities
- political movements
- opportunistic soldiers

Rich powers describe them as invasions.

The migrants describe them as survival.

Both descriptions contain truth.

---

## 13. Rome’s migration policy

Rome initially attempts a controlled integration policy.

Migrants can enter through:

- labour contracts
- military service
- agricultural settlement
- provincial sponsorship
- public works programmes

Long-term residents can earn citizenship.

This policy supports Roman expansion.

Many frontier regions become loyal because Rome offers legal status and infrastructure.

But the scale eventually exceeds the system’s capacity.

By year 116,900, millions arrive annually during bad years.

Roman cities struggle to provide:

- housing
- sanitation
- schools
- food
- employment
- political representation

Some provinces close their borders in defiance of central law.

Others recruit migrants into private industrial forces or provincial armies.

The issue divides the Republic.

---

## 14. Roman political factions

Four major blocs emerge.

## The Restorationists

They support continued expansion, industrialisation and eventual planetary unity.

They favour integrating migrants as citizens and workers.

## The Civic Traditionalists

They support the Republic but want slower expansion and stronger local identity.

They demand strict limits on migration.

## The Industrial Front

It represents major manufacturers, infrastructure authorities and scientific institutions.

It wants cheap labour, technological freedom and rapid growth.

## The Resilience Coalition

It warns that Rome is becoming dangerously dependent on complex systems.

It supports migration only where food, water and low-energy backups are adequate.

These blocs repeatedly form and break coalitions.

No faction controls the Republic permanently.

---

## 15. The rise of frontier militarism

Rome’s frontier armies grow.

Originally designed to defend borders and maintain roads, they become permanent political actors.

They manage:

- refugee camps
- emergency food distribution
- quarantine zones
- migration corridors
- border settlements
- anti-smuggling operations

Commanders gain influence because civilian institutions often cannot respond quickly enough.

Some frontier generals become popular heroes.

Others establish corrupt local regimes.

The Senate fears military domination but cannot reduce the army while tensions are rising.

---

## 16. The technological frontier approaches space

By year **116,820**, Terra’s average IRF intensity has fallen to approximately **63%**.

Near the Wound of Return, local intensity is lower.

In those regions, chemical rockets become barely viable.

They are still highly unreliable.

The IRF disrupts:

- combustion stability
- high-pressure fuel flow
- guidance systems
- structural tolerances
- ignition timing
- electrical controls

Early rockets frequently:

- explode on launch
- lose thrust
- veer off course
- disintegrate at altitude
- suffer control failure
- ignite prematurely

But some succeed.

Small unmanned rockets begin reaching the upper atmosphere.

This changes world politics immediately.

---

## 17. The beginning of the space race

The first successful high-altitude rocket belongs to the Pacific city-state of **Aster Bay** in year 116,829.

It reaches an altitude of approximately 170 kilometres before falling into the ocean.

It carries no useful payload.

It transmits only a few minutes of data.

Nevertheless, it crosses the conventional boundary of space.

Every major power reacts.

Rome creates the **Officium Caeleste**, the Office of the Heavens.

The Eastern Mandate establishes a central launch authority.

The Atlantic League funds competing private and state programmes.

The Pacific states multiply their existing rocket projects.

The Rhine Compact begins a smaller but highly sophisticated programme.

The race to the Moon begins before anyone can reliably place an object in orbit.

---

## 18. Why the race is more intense than the first space race

The Moon is not merely a symbolic destination.

It contains the ruins of an earlier civilisation.

Every major power believes lunar sites may contain:

- preserved industrial machinery
- advanced archives
- sealed biological vaults
- dormant computers
- medical knowledge
- materials unavailable on Terra
- records of the Great Release
- evidence about the IRF

The first state to reach the Moon could gain an enormous technological advantage.

It might recover centuries of development in a single expedition.

This makes the space race existential.

No power is willing to allow another uncontested access.

---

## 19. The lunar claims crisis

Before anyone can reach orbit reliably, governments begin claiming lunar territory.

Rome argues that the Moon is the common inheritance of humanity.

The Atlantic League supports commercial access and salvage rights.

The Eastern Mandate claims that ancient lunar sites should be administered by a unified technical authority—meaning itself.

The Pacific Cavity states claim leadership because their technology makes renewed spaceflight possible.

Mars, if its weak signals are understood correctly, asserts that the Moon’s heritage belongs to all surviving human worlds.

No agreement is reached.

Maps are published dividing the Moon into proposed zones.

These maps have no practical meaning yet.

They nevertheless intensify tensions.

---

## 20. First orbital attempts

Between years 116,850 and 116,900, more than three hundred orbital launch attempts are made.

Fewer than twenty reach a stable orbit.

Most payloads are tiny.

Successful launches carry:

- radio beacons
- cameras
- radiation instruments
- IRF sensors
- primitive navigation equipment

Orbit remains difficult because rockets must operate continuously near the upper edge of reliable energy concentration.

A vehicle that works during ground testing may fail at altitude as local field conditions change.

Even successful satellites often stop functioning within days.

---

## 21. The first artificial satellite of the new age

In year **116,861**, the Eastern Mandate places the first verified artificial satellite of the new industrial recovery into orbit.

The satellite is called **Continuity One**.

It completes 143 orbits before its transmitter fails.

The achievement shocks Rome.

Roman newspapers accuse the government of complacency.

The Senate grants emergency funds to the Officium Caeleste.

The Atlantic League’s financial markets surge around launch companies.

The Pacific states announce that they had achieved an unverified orbit earlier.

The dispute continues for generations.

---

## 22. Sabotage begins

Because launch technology is so fragile, sabotage is easy to disguise as accident.

A small defect can destroy an entire vehicle.

Methods include:

- contaminating fuel
- weakening pressure seals
- altering guidance tables
- damaging relay contacts
- introducing impurities into metal
- bribing technicians
- stealing weather data
- corrupting IRF measurements

Every power begins infiltrating rival programmes.

Launch failures become politically suspicious.

Engineers are interrogated.

Security expands.

Scientific exchange declines.

This slows progress considerably.

---

## 23. The Roman lunar programme

Rome’s lunar programme is the largest and most publicly visible.

It builds launch centres around:

- the Mediterranean
- equatorial allied territories
- Pacific research enclaves
- high-altitude plateaus

The Republic attempts to combine resources from hundreds of provinces.

Its advantages are:

- population
- industrial scale
- engineering tradition
- administrative capacity
- access to Latin Nova scientific literature

Its weaknesses are:

- political debate
- provincial rivalry
- corruption
- budget disputes
- military interference
- uneven access to the weakest IRF zones

Every Roman faction wants control of the programme.

This makes it powerful but inefficient.

---

## 24. The Eastern Mandate’s programme

The Mandate’s programme is centrally controlled.

Its launch authority can redirect enormous resources quickly.

It possesses:

- disciplined engineering teams
- secret research facilities
- standardised manufacturing
- centralised testing
- a large military budget

Its disadvantages are:

- fear of reporting failure
- suppression of dissent
- poor communication between departments
- political punishment for delays
- excessive secrecy

Several catastrophic accidents occur because engineers conceal problems from superiors.

Official records minimise the death toll.

Foreign intelligence estimates that tens of thousands die across the programme’s first two centuries.

---

## 25. The Atlantic commercial race

The Atlantic League allows multiple organisations to compete.

Some are state-backed.

Others are private companies, universities or merchant coalitions.

This produces rapid innovation.

It also produces:

- fraud
- reckless launches
- duplicated effort
- financial bubbles
- abandoned projects
- poor safety standards

Investors become obsessed with lunar salvage.

Companies sell shares based on proposed claims to lunar cities they cannot reach.

Several speculative crashes damage the League’s economy.

But its engineers pioneer reusable launch stages and ocean-based launch systems.

---

## 26. The Pacific advantage

The Pacific Cavity states have the best physical conditions for launches.

Near the Wound, IRF intensity is lower than the global average.

Their engines can run at higher pressure.

Their rockets can carry more fuel.

Their guidance systems are more reliable.

But the region is politically fragmented.

No single state possesses Rome’s resources or the Mandate’s central command.

Attempts to create a unified Pacific lunar authority repeatedly fail.

Rival cities sabotage one another almost as often as foreign powers sabotage them.

The region remains technologically ahead but strategically divided.

---

## 27. The first animals in orbit

By year **116,905**, several powers have launched animals into orbit.

Most die from:

- life-support failure
- heat
- acceleration injuries
- radiation
- recovery failures

The first living creatures successfully returned from orbit are small mammals launched by a Pacific consortium.

Rome later returns a primate-like animal safely.

The Eastern Mandate sends several human volunteers on suborbital flights, though most records remain secret.

The ethical debate becomes intense.

Some argue that no person should fly until rescue systems exist.

Others argue that delay will hand the Moon to rivals.

---

## 28. The first human in space

In year **116,932**, a Mandate pilot becomes the first confirmed human of the new age to cross into space.

The flight is suborbital.

It lasts twenty-one minutes.

The pilot survives but suffers severe injuries.

The Mandate announces the achievement only after several months.

Rome responds by launching its own crewed suborbital mission within four years.

The Atlantic League follows.

The Pacific states dispute both records and claim an earlier unconfirmed flight.

From this point onward, human spaceflight exists.

But orbital human flight remains exceptionally dangerous.

---

## 29. No one reaches the Moon

The Moon is much harder than reaching orbit.

A lunar mission requires:

- heavy payload capacity
- reliable orbital operations
- navigation over several days
- life support
- course correction
- lunar landing
- ascent from the surface
- return to Terra
- protection against sabotage
- political willingness to risk enormous expense

No power can combine all these reliably.

Each time one approaches the threshold, something intervenes.

Sometimes it is technology.

Sometimes economics.

Sometimes war.

Sometimes sabotage.

---

## 30. The failed Roman expedition

In year **116,978**, Rome assembles the first vehicle publicly intended to send humans around the Moon.

The mission is called **Luna Prima**.

It is not designed to land.

Its crew of four would circle the Moon and return.

Three weeks before launch, a fire destroys the upper propulsion assembly.

Investigators find evidence of deliberate contamination in an oxygen system.

Rome blames the Eastern Mandate.

The Mandate denies involvement and suggests internal Roman corruption.

Several Roman officials are arrested.

One dies in custody.

The mission is delayed for decades.

---

## 31. The Mandate disaster

In year **117,006**, the Eastern Mandate secretly attempts a crewed lunar flyby.

The vehicle reaches orbit.

Its translunar stage ignites unevenly.

The craft enters a highly elliptical orbit but lacks enough control to return safely.

The crew remains alive for six days.

Their final transmissions are intercepted by several powers.

The Mandate initially denies the mission existed.

The deaths become public years later.

The disaster intensifies demands for rescue cooperation.

No binding international agreement follows.

---

## 32. Economic strain of the race

The space race consumes extraordinary resources.

Launch programmes require:

- the purest metals
- specialised fuels
- precision manufacturing
- large test facilities
- thousands of engineers
- extensive security
- transport infrastructure
- scientific education

These resources are diverted from:

- housing
- irrigation
- famine relief
- public health
- migration management
- environmental restoration

Poor regions see wealthy powers spending fortunes on lunar competition while refugees lack food.

The Moon becomes a symbol of both human aspiration and political injustice.

---

## 33. Anti-space movements

Several political movements oppose the race.

They argue that:

- no one should reach the Moon while Terra remains divided
- lunar salvage will intensify inequality
- launch accidents threaten populated regions
- space programmes are military projects disguised as science
- resources should address migration and environmental collapse

Some anti-space movements are peaceful.

Others sabotage launch sites.

A few cooperate with rival intelligence services.

Governments increasingly treat opposition to space programmes as a security threat.

---

## 34. The militarisation of orbit

Once satellites become somewhat reliable, military uses follow.

Powers launch:

- reconnaissance cameras
- radio relays
- navigation beacons
- weather instruments
- IRF monitors

No one admits to orbital weapons.

All major powers study them.

Because satellites are fragile, anti-satellite attacks are relatively easy.

Ground-based rockets can destroy low-orbit objects.

Sabotage can disable control stations.

By year 117,020, orbital assets are frequently attacked during political crises.

This creates debris and makes later launches more dangerous.

---

## 35. The Kessler danger of the new age

Terra’s renewed orbital environment becomes contaminated with debris before a stable space infrastructure exists.

Failed rockets, shattered satellites and collision fragments accumulate.

The amount is far below that of the old industrial era, but tracking systems are much poorer.

Every launch must cross uncertain debris fields.

Several missions are lost to impacts.

Engineers warn that uncontrolled competition may close access to orbit.

The powers agree to limited tracking cooperation.

They continue destroying one another’s satellites.

---

## 36. The global migration emergency

While the space race accelerates, conditions worsen across several poorer regions.

A sequence of environmental and economic crises occurs between years 116,950 and 117,060:

- prolonged droughts in continental interiors
- river-course changes
- soil exhaustion
- mining contamination
- livestock epidemics
- collapse of regional credit systems
- rising food prices
- political revolutions
- border closures

More than **150 million people** are displaced over a century.

Some move internally.

Others cross international borders.

A growing number move in organised armed columns.

---

## 37. The Southern Marches crisis

One of the largest movements occurs along Rome’s southern and southeastern frontiers.

Millions leave regions suffering from:

- water scarcity
- debt
- failed harvests
- political fragmentation
- foreign resource extraction

Rome cannot admit everyone immediately.

Border provinces close crossings.

Refugee settlements grow into temporary cities.

Disease spreads.

Local armed groups begin escorting migrants through Roman defences.

Several Roman frontier towns are seized.

Rome deploys legions.

The resulting conflict lasts nearly twenty years.

Roman histories call it the **Southern Marches War**.

Migrant traditions call it the **War of Entry**.

---

## 38. Rome’s response

Rome eventually combines force with integration.

Armed groups that surrender are offered:

- settlement land
- citizenship pathways
- public works employment
- military service
- relocation to underpopulated provinces

Groups that continue attacking are treated as hostile armies.

Hundreds of thousands die.

Millions are resettled.

The Republic survives the crisis but becomes more militarised.

Frontier commanders receive permanent emergency authority.

The Senate promises to revoke it later.

In many regions, it does not.

---

## 39. Invasions of the Atlantic League

The Atlantic League faces a different pattern.

Migrants arrive through:

- ocean crossings
- island routes
- commercial ports
- military transport
- seized merchant vessels

Some poor coastal states sponsor mass migration deliberately to pressure the League.

Others lose control of armed fleets.

Several League ports are overwhelmed.

Merchant companies hire private armies.

Naval battles occur between state fleets, migrant flotillas and commercial security forces.

The League’s response is inconsistent.

Some cities integrate newcomers.

Others expel them violently.

This fragmentation weakens the League politically.

---

## 40. The Mandate’s border wars

The Eastern Mandate seals its borders more aggressively.

It builds:

- fortified rail corridors
- controlled agricultural zones
- relocation camps
- surveillance networks
- military barriers

Large migrant populations are redirected into labour settlements.

Resistance becomes common.

Several peripheral provinces rebel.

The Mandate suppresses them but at enormous cost.

Its central system appears stable from outside.

Internally, resentment grows.

---

## 41. The ideology of dispossession

Among displaced populations, a new political ideology spreads.

Its followers argue that industrial powers have accumulated wealth by exploiting poorer regions and therefore have no moral right to exclude those regions’ people.

The movement has many names.

The most common is the **Common Inheritance doctrine**.

It claims that:

- Terra belongs to all humanity
- stable land and water cannot be monopolised
- industrial technology is the inheritance of every civilisation
- borders cannot override survival
- resource-rich regions must accept redistribution

Some movements pursue legal reform.

Others advocate mass occupation.

More radical groups call for the overthrow of all existing industrial powers.

---

## 42. Rich-region reaction

In wealthier states, the Common Inheritance doctrine is viewed as a threat to property and sovereignty.

Countermovements demand:

- fortified borders
- compulsory identification
- restrictions on citizenship
- military settlement of frontier zones
- deportation
- birth controls in poorer regions
- control over migrant labour

Political violence increases.

Assassinations, riots and border massacres become common.

The gap between humanitarian ideals and state behaviour widens.

---

## 43. Rome’s expansion during the crisis

Rome grows partly because neighbouring states seek protection from migration, war and economic collapse.

Some join voluntarily.

Others accept Roman troops and later discover that the troops do not leave.

Rome absorbs several frontier states through emergency treaties.

It also acquires new territories after defeating hostile coalitions.

By year 117,110, Roman influence extends far beyond its earlier Mediterranean core.

But expansion does not mean stability.

Rome now governs more languages, ecological regions, religions and political traditions than ever before.

Its internal tensions grow alongside its territory.

---

## 44. The Republic’s democratic strain

Roman elections become increasingly bitter.

Campaigns accuse opponents of:

- betraying citizens for migrants
- abandoning humanitarian law
- serving industrial families
- weakening the military
- wasting resources on the Moon
- surrendering to foreign powers
- undermining the Republic

Political militias appear.

Some are associated with industrial unions.

Others with provincial parties, veteran groups or migration movements.

The Civic Council warns that electoral violence is becoming normalised.

The Senate debates emergency constitutional reforms.

No consensus emerges.

---

## 45. The Great Roman compromise

In year **117,067**, the Republic adopts the **Concord of Peoples**.

It attempts to balance integration and control.

The law provides:

- limited annual migration quotas
- emergency admission during ecological disasters
- citizenship through service and residence
- provincial funding for housing and sanitation
- penalties for exploitative labour contracts
- stronger border enforcement
- mandatory redistribution of some migrants across provinces

The compromise satisfies no one completely.

It reduces immediate chaos.

It does not resolve the underlying pressures.

---

## 46. The Moon race becomes openly military

By year 117,050, all major space programmes are integrated with military planning.

Rockets developed for orbital launches can also carry weapons across continents.

Guidance research supports both lunar missions and strategic missiles.

Launch sites become military targets.

Powers disperse their programmes across underground facilities and remote regions.

A successful lunar vehicle would imply the ability to strike almost anywhere on Terra.

The Moon race and the arms race become inseparable.

---

## 47. The orbital crisis of 117,081

A Roman reconnaissance satellite is destroyed while passing over Eastern Mandate territory.

Rome accuses the Mandate.

The Mandate claims the satellite failed naturally.

Roman naval forces move into contested waters.

The Mandate mobilises frontier armies.

The Atlantic League raises insurance rates and begins escorting trade convoys.

For several months, war appears imminent.

A neutral Pacific investigation finds evidence of deliberate attack but cannot identify the perpetrator.

Some later historians suspect the satellite was destroyed by a third power seeking conflict.

The crisis ends without war.

Trust does not recover.

---

## 48. The Pacific launch-site wars

Competition among the Pacific Cavity states becomes increasingly violent.

Because launch conditions there are superior, every major power seeks bases in the region.

Local states divide into pro-Roman, pro-Mandate, pro-League and neutral factions.

A series of coups, assassinations and limited wars erupts.

Launch centres are bombed.

Scientists are kidnapped.

Research archives are stolen.

One city threatens to destroy its own IRF observatory rather than surrender it.

The wars slow technological progress by decades.

They also spread rocket expertise across Terra as displaced engineers flee.

---

## 49. Why no Moon landing occurs

By year 117,110, the basic components of a crewed lunar expedition exist separately.

Humanity can:

- launch small crews into orbit
- keep them alive for several days
- perform limited orbital manoeuvres
- transmit across lunar distances
- build powerful chemical rockets
- recover capsules from orbit

It cannot reliably combine all of these at the scale required for a landing.

The obstacles are partly physical:

- IRF instability
- insufficient payload
- unreliable engines
- limited guidance
- poor life support
- orbital debris

But the decisive obstacles are political:

- programmes are duplicated rather than shared
- sabotage destroys prototypes
- military secrecy prevents scientific exchange
- budgets rise and collapse with governments
- wars interrupt supply chains
- poorer regions resist the diversion of resources
- rival powers refuse joint rescue arrangements

Humanity is technically approaching the Moon.

Politically, it is moving away from cooperation.

---

## 50. The closest attempt

In year **117,096**, an Atlantic consortium launches an uncrewed lunar lander.

The vehicle reaches lunar orbit.

It begins descent near an ancient industrial site.

During the final phase, its guidance system receives contradictory commands.

The lander crashes.

Evidence later suggests that its communication network had been infiltrated.

No one proves which state was responsible.

The consortium collapses financially.

Its technical staff disperse to Rome, the Pacific states and the Rhine Compact.

This remains the closest anyone comes to a controlled lunar landing during the period.

---

## 51. Mars over these five centuries

Mars continues surviving.

Its population remains between approximately **120,000 and 200,000**.

The background IRF intensity around Mars declines slightly but remains far stronger than on Terra.

Martian civilisation changes slowly.

Its priorities remain:

- habitat integrity
- ecological stability
- water security
- population balance
- preservation of knowledge

Martians detect Terra’s growing radio activity.

They hear fragments of:

- political speeches
- launch announcements
- military transmissions
- scientific reports
- distress signals

From Mars, Terra appears powerful and unstable.

Some Martian communities regard Terra’s space race as hopeful.

Others see it as evidence that Terrans are repeating the old cycle.

---

## 52. Martian participation in the Moon race

Mars cannot contribute materially.

It lacks the industrial capacity for interplanetary flight.

But Martian archives contain valuable information about:

- ancient spacecraft
- low-energy life support
- pressure systems
- closed ecosystems
- long-duration habitation

Weak Martian transmissions occasionally provide technical suggestions.

Several are incorporated into Terran spacecraft design.

Rome publicly credits Martian knowledge.

The Eastern Mandate classifies similar transmissions.

No crewed contact occurs.

Mars remains physically isolated but intellectually relevant.

---

## 53. Europa over these five centuries

Europan existence remains confirmed through intermittent signals.

The messages are rare and difficult to decode.

They suggest that Europa’s population remains between **100,000 and 300,000**.

Several oceanic communities disappear after hydrothermal changes.

Others form new alliances.

Europan technology remains biological and hydrothermal.

The space race on Terra has almost no practical effect on them.

They are too distant and the Jovian IRF remains too strong.

But Europans eventually understand that Terra has regained limited spaceflight.

Their surviving messages include a repeated warning:

> Do not build a civilisation that dies when the machines stop.

The phrase becomes famous on Terra.

Different political factions interpret it differently.

---

## 54. Titan over these five centuries

Titan remains stable at roughly **12–18 million people**.

Its societies are only faintly aware of Terra’s transformations.

Long-distance signals arrive irregularly.

Titanian thinkers are divided.

Some welcome the possibility that Terra may eventually restore interplanetary contact.

Others fear that renewed Terran expansion will bring:

- political domination
- ecological disruption
- military rivalry
- technological dependency

Titan’s cavern federations continue focusing on slow ecological growth.

They are materially less advanced than Terra.

They are socially more stable.

---

## 55. The extrasolar colony

The surviving extrasolar colony continues developing independently.

Its population grows into the tens of millions.

Because the full-strength IRF still affects its star system, it remains low-energy.

Its civilisation combines:

- agriculture
- water and wind power
- mechanical computation
- advanced science
- ecological management
- inherited knowledge of spaceflight

It has no communication with Sol.

Its people increasingly regard Sol as an ancestral world that may no longer contain living humans.

---

## 56. The fleeing colony ship

The surviving ship that continues racing the IRF remains beyond the affected frontier.

It has not yet detected the inner weakening.

Its society has existed aboard the vessel for so long that original mission structures have evolved into permanent institutions.

The ship possesses:

- a closed biosphere
- advanced propulsion
- automated repair
- controlled population
- extensive historical archives

Its inhabitants debate whether they are still fleeing toward a destination or whether endless movement is itself their civilisation.

The future discovery of the weakening field remains far ahead.

---

## 57. Terra’s population in year 117,110

Terra’s total population reaches approximately **3.8–4.2 billion**.

Growth has slowed in poorer regions due to:

- famine
- displacement
- conflict
- environmental decline
- disease

Industrial regions continue growing through both natural increase and migration.

The population is more urban than five centuries earlier.

But hundreds of millions live in:

- informal settlements
- refugee corridors
- frontier zones
- industrial slums
- temporary agricultural colonies

Material progress and human insecurity exist side by side.

---

## 58. Technology in year 117,110

Terra’s average IRF intensity is approximately **60%**.

Near the Wound of Return, local conditions are somewhat weaker.

Terra now possesses:

- extensive continental railway systems
- large regional electric networks
- sophisticated industrial chemistry
- powered road vehicles in wealthy regions
- high-altitude aircraft and limited heavier-than-air aviation
- human suborbital and orbital flight
- artificial satellites
- global radio networks
- advanced electromechanical computation
- mass-produced antibiotics
- improved surgery
- early automated factories
- powerful chemical rockets

It still lacks reliable:

- semiconductor industry
- nuclear power
- fusion
- large turbine aviation
- high-density digital computing
- inexpensive orbital launch
- crewed lunar capability
- interplanetary transport

---

## 59. Aviation

Aircraft become possible but remain constrained.

Most use:

- low-output combustion engines
- hybrid electric systems
- gliders with powered ascent
- large airships
- mechanically simple controls

Airships remain more common than aeroplanes for cargo and long-distance travel.

Military aircraft exist but are expensive and unreliable.

The richest powers maintain air fleets.

Poorer states cannot.

This adds another layer to global inequality.

---

## 60. Daily life in wealthy regions

In Rome, the Atlantic League, the Mandate and advanced Pacific states, many urban citizens have access to:

- electric lighting
- piped water
- radio
- mechanised transport
- manufactured medicine
- public schools
- industrial employment
- refrigeration
- printed and transmitted news
- limited personal vehicles

Life expectancy may exceed ninety years in the best regions.

Education is widespread.

But daily life is increasingly shaped by:

- security checks
- political propaganda
- migration debates
- military mobilisation
- industrial labour disputes
- fear of sabotage

Prosperity does not produce peace.

---

## 61. Daily life in poorer regions

In less-industrialised territories, most people still depend on:

- small-scale farming
- animal transport
- communal wells
- local markets
- manual labour
- seasonal migration
- limited clinics

They know richer lifestyles exist.

Radio makes inequality visible.

Migrants return with stories of electric cities, hospitals and machines.

This raises expectations and resentment.

The poor are not isolated from modernity.

They are excluded from much of it.

That exclusion becomes politically explosive.

---

## 62. The approach of major war

By year 117,110, nearly every major power is preparing for war while publicly claiming to seek peace.

Rome fears encirclement by the Eastern Mandate and hostile Atlantic factions.

The Mandate fears Roman expansion and internal revolt.

The Atlantic League fears both continental powers.

The Pacific states fear foreign occupation.

The Rhine Compact fears being absorbed into one of the larger systems.

Poor-region coalitions threaten resource embargoes and mass migration campaigns.

Military budgets rise.

Railways are designed for troop movement.

Rocket programmes become strategic weapons programmes.

Food reserves expand.

Civil-defence systems are built.

Governments map potential front lines.

---

## 63. The likely causes of war

No single issue guarantees conflict.

Several issues overlap:

- competition for Pacific launch zones
- control of mineral regions
- migration across fortified borders
- Roman expansion
- Mandate interventions
- Atlantic commercial dominance
- sabotage of space programmes
- orbital attacks
- water rights
- ideological conflict
- lunar claims

Any one of these could trigger a wider war.

The greatest danger is that a local crisis will activate alliance obligations.

The world resembles a system under mechanical strain.

No part has broken yet.

Every part is connected to the others.

---

## 64. Rome in year 117,110

Rome has grown enormously, but it has not unified Terra.

It is the strongest global political project, not the only one.

The Republic possesses:

- nearly a billion citizens and associated inhabitants
- the largest integrated railway system
- one of the world’s strongest armies
- a major space programme
- extensive industrial capacity
- Latin Nova cultural influence
- strong archives and universities
- a powerful but contentious democratic system

It also faces:

- migration pressure
- frontier militarisation
- provincial nationalism
- industrial inequality
- political militias
- corruption
- conflict over lunar spending
- rivalry with the Mandate
- distrust from independent states

Rome’s project of restoring all ages remains alive.

Its success is not assured.

---

## 65. The Moon at the end of the period

The Moon remains silent.

Its dead cities circle Terra as they have for millennia.

Through telescopes and orbital cameras, humanity can now identify:

- collapsed surface structures
- ancient landing fields
- sealed entrances
- transport lines
- damaged industrial zones

No living person has reached them.

No new human footprint marks the lunar surface.

Every major power claims it will be first.

Every major power fears that another will arrive first.

The technical distance has become small enough to imagine and large enough to kill.

---

## 66. End of the five-century period

On the last day of year **117,110**, several events occur within hours of one another.

A Roman launch vehicle explodes during testing.

A Mandate army begins exercises near a disputed frontier.

An Atlantic convoy refuses inspection by a Roman patrol.

A refugee column breaks through a fortified border in a drought-stricken region.

A Pacific observatory reports evidence that an unidentified satellite has manoeuvred near one of its own.

None of these events begins the great war.

But historians later identify them as symptoms of the same condition.

Terra has regained the power to reshape itself.

It has not regained the unity to decide how.

The IRF continues weakening.

The rockets rise higher.

The borders harden.

The refugee columns grow.

And above the increasingly armed planet, the Moon waits—close enough to inspire every power, but still beyond the reach of them all.
