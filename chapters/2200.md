# 2200 — Year 2200

## Population

Perhaps **1–2 billion people**, but probably not much more.

Agriculture continues to limit population. Without tractors, synthetic fertilisers, refrigeration, combines, and railways, every additional person requires a considerable quantity of human labour for sustenance.

---

## Cities

Cities might finally reach one or two million inhabitants, but only in exceptional locations with:
- excellent rivers
- canals
- fertile surrounding land
- advanced sanitation

No multi-decamillion coastal megacities.

---

## Knowledge

This is where the largest difference appears.

By 2200, scholars would have had **another 400 years** of uninterrupted scientific progress.

They might comprehend:
- relativity
- quantum mechanics
- plate tectonics
- DNA
- evolution
- neuroscience
- climate systems

Their textbooks might be almost identical to ours.

They simply could not construct much of the technology those theories enable.

---

## Computing

This is fascinating.

Electronic computers would almost certainly never exist.

Instead one might observe incredibly sophisticated:
- mechanical computers
- hydraulic computers
- optical analogue computers
- enormous calculating engines

Consider Babbage's Difference Engine after another 300 years of refinement.

Governments and universities might possess room-sized mechanical computers capable of astonishing calculations.

---

## Communication

Perhaps something like:
- global telegraph networks
- pneumatic message systems within cities
- mechanical fax-like devices
- optical communication towers

Information could travel worldwide in hours or days instead of months.

Nevertheless, nothing analogous to the Internet.

---

## Transportation

Transport improves slowly.

Potentially:
- perfected sailing ships
- lightweight horse-drawn vehicles
- extensive canal systems
- improved bicycles
- maybe electrically powered trams if local-scale generators become practical

But:
- no aeroplanes
- no container ships
- no motorways
- no high-speed rail

Atlantic crossings continue requiring weeks.

---

## Medicine

Medicine could become surprisingly sophisticated.

Doctors might be aware of:
- every human pathogen
- genetics
- nutrition
- epidemiology

But they would struggle to manufacture:
- MRI scanners
- dialysis machines
- pacemakers
- modern pharmaceuticals

Life expectancy at birth might reach **55–68 years**, but rarely exceed seventy.

---

## Daily life

A household might have:
- piped water
- flush toilets
- gas lighting
- perhaps limited electrical lighting
- beautiful handcrafted furniture
- mechanical clocks accurate to fractions of a second

But:
- no television
- no microwave
- no refrigerator
- no washing machine
- no vacuum cleaner

Daily chores continue consuming much of the day.

---

## Economy

Most people would still labour in:
- farming
- construction
- textiles
- food production
- transport
- crafts

Maybe only 10–20% of society could specialise in science, art, law, or administration.

---

## Warfare

Weapons improve through chemistry and metallurgy.

Potentially:
- advanced rifles
- precision artillery
- armoured wagons
- primitive rockets

But not:
- tanks
- fighter aircraft
- drones
- missiles
- nuclear weapons

Wars become more accurate rather than vastly more destructive.

---

## Space

People might comprehend orbital mechanics perfectly.

They could predict satellite trajectories.

They could calculate voyages to Mars.

They simply could not construct rockets capable of getting there.

Consider comprehending precisely how a mature heavy-lift orbital launcher operates while the largest machine workshop is powered by a hydraulic wheel.

---

## What would surprise us?

The primary historical implication would be **how intelligent the civilisation appears**.

One might enter a university and hear a lecture concerning:
- black holes
- quantum fields
- genetic inheritance
- continental drift

Then exit and observe:
- horses delivering the mail
- blacksmiths forging tools
- women washing clothes by hand
- barges carrying grain down canals

Knowledge has advanced centuries beyond the technology available to apply it.

---

## It might become a civilisation of masterpieces

Because machines never become inexpensive, nearly everything is constructed to endure.

Furniture lasts centuries.

Clothes are repaired dozens of times.

Structures are designed for hundreds of years.

Craftsmanship becomes extraordinarily refined.

Instead of billions of identical plastic objects, the world is occupied by individually crafted items.

---

### An interesting possibility: a "thinking civilisation"

Such a society might eventually devote far more effort to mathematics, philosophy, biology, and astronomy than we have, simply because it lacks the ability to direct vast resources into industrial production. By 2200 they might comprehend numerous scientific mysteries that we continue debating today, yet still travel predominantly by horse and sail.

So 2200 would not appear analogous to "the 1700s with superior science". It would appear analogous to an alternate branch of civilisation: intellectually on par with, or perhaps even ahead of us in some theoretical fields, but materially constrained by the absence of abundant mechanical power and mass manufacturing. It would be a world where cognition has advanced rapidly, but human hands never acquired machines sufficiently robust to realise all of its ideas.
