# 117160-117260 — After the War

## 117,160

The Roman Republic controls:
- all its pre-war territories
- all former integration provinces
- large portions of the former Eastern Mandate
- several wartime accessions
- strategic corridors linking its continental regions

Its population is approximately **2.2–2.5 billion**, though the first postwar censuses are incomplete.

Terra as a whole has roughly **3.6–3.9 billion people**.

The discrepancy reflects the enormous death toll of the Unification War, reduced birth rates during the conflict and continuing mortality from famine, disease and displacement.

The Atlantic League remains outside Roman control, along with:
- several independent coastal republics
- former Mandate successor states
- remote neutral federations
- island polities
- a number of culturally distinct inland nations

Rome is unquestionably the strongest state on Terra, but it does not yet rule the whole planet.

---

## 1. The IRF during the century

The long-term decline continues normally.

Terra’s average intensity falls from approximately:
- 59.5% in 117,160
- 59.0% in 117,210
- 58.5% in 117,260

The change is small but technologically meaningful.

Near the Wound of Return, local intensities remain several percentage points lower than the planetary average.

Approximate levels by year 117,260:

| Location | Approximate IRF intensity |
| --- | --- |
| Wound of Return | 50–53% |
| Terra average | 58.5% |
| Near-Terran orbit | 61–64% |
| Luna | 67–71% |
| Mars | 86–91% |
| Asteroid belt | 93–97% |
| Europa/Jupiter | 98–100% |
| Titan/Saturn | ~100% |
| Extrasolar affected regions | 100% |
| Beyond the expanding frontier | 0% |

The field still expands outward beyond the Solar System, but its inner regions continue thinning.

No periodic IRF burst occurs during this century. The next one remains due in year **118,110**.

---

## 2. The first postwar decade, 117,160–117,170

The first ten years are dominated by emergency reconstruction.

Rome’s priorities are:
- feeding displaced populations
- restoring water systems
- repairing railways
- demobilising armies
- clearing chemical contamination
- rebuilding hospitals
- reopening schools
- standardising administration
- integrating former Mandate territories

Millions of soldiers return home.

Many discover that their villages no longer exist.

Others settle in the territories where they fought.

The Republic creates vast veteran and refugee settlement programmes, often placing people in underpopulated agricultural districts or ruined industrial cities.

This eases immediate homelessness, but also changes regional demographics permanently.

---

## 3. Demobilisation and the danger of military politics

Rome had promised that the army would not govern the peace.

Keeping that promise proves difficult.

Wartime commanders control:
- railway districts
- food warehouses
- local police
- communications centres
- heavy industry
- emergency courts

Some surrender authority willingly.

Others insist that civilian institutions are too weak.

A few attempt to convert military commands into permanent regional governments.

The most serious crisis occurs in **117,164**, when three senior generals refuse demobilisation orders in former Mandate territory.

The crisis ends without major war.

The Senate offers amnesty to ordinary soldiers, isolates the commanders politically and cuts their supplies through civilian railway unions.

Two generals surrender.

The third attempts a coup and is arrested by his own officers.

This event establishes an important precedent:

The Republic will tolerate military prestige, but not military sovereignty.

---

## 4. The new constitution takes effect

The rewritten constitution is implemented gradually.

The most difficult changes are:
- unifying legal codes
- removing hereditary rulers
- converting old integration territories into ordinary provinces
- standardising taxation
- establishing uniform citizenship
- replacing military courts
- creating national elections in devastated regions

The first elections under the new constitution occur in **117,168**.

Turnout is uneven but immense.

In some former Mandate provinces, voting is the first open political participation in living memory.

The election produces a conservative but democratic governing coalition.

It supports:
- social order
- cultural continuity
- cautious reform
- strong national institutions
- restrained military spending
- long-term reconstruction

It does not seek to weaken elections or civil rights.

This becomes the beginning of Rome’s postwar conservative period.

---

## 5. What Roman conservatism means

Roman conservatism in this century is not primarily opposition to democracy.

It is a reaction against instability.

Its supporters believe the war resulted partly from:
- uncontrolled migration
- unequal development
- rapid industrial dependency
- weak shared identity
- fragmented law
- historical amnesia
- political extremism

They therefore emphasise:
- institutional continuity
- linguistic stability
- civic ritual
- preservation of monuments
- resilient infrastructure
- archive construction
- ecological restoration
- cautious expansion

Rome still innovates technologically.

But it increasingly tries to bind innovation to permanent institutions.

---

## 6. The Latin Preservation Programme

In **117,171**, the Republic establishes the **Institutum Latinitatis Perpetuae**, the Institute of Perpetual Latin.

Its purpose is to preserve the current form of Latin Nova indefinitely.

The Republic fears that ordinary language drift could eventually divide its citizens linguistically, especially across remote provinces.

The programme creates:
- an official national dictionary
- a fixed grammar
- standard pronunciation guides
- preserved audio records
- regional teaching academies
- standard technical terminology
- periodic language examinations for officials
- archived examples of formal and informal speech

Latin is allowed to develop new vocabulary.

Its core grammar, pronunciation and written structure are not supposed to change.

New terms must be formed according to established rules.

The Republic distinguishes between:
- Permanent Latin, used in law, science, national education and archives
- regional spoken varieties, which may change naturally
- provincial secondary languages

Over time, ordinary people still develop accents and slang.

But a citizen from year 117,260 can read national documents from 117,160 without difficulty.

The programme’s ultimate goal is more ambitious:

A Roman citizen ten thousand years in the future should still understand the constitution directly.

---

## 7. Rebuilding the monuments of all ages

In **117,174**, Rome creates the **Restoration of the Works of Humanity Programme**.

Its mission is to identify, preserve and eventually reconstruct great buildings and monuments located within Roman territory.

The programme includes structures from every era:
- ancient temples
- forums
- aqueducts
- cathedrals
- mosques
- palaces
- civic halls
- observatories
- Long Age archive complexes
- industrial-era towers
- Great Release launch facilities
- Second Breaking shelters
- post-impact memorials
- wartime ruins

The purpose is not to recreate one idealised civilisation.

Rome wants to preserve the visible evidence of all human ages.

Each major city establishes a historical survey.

Structures are classified as:
- intact and preservable
- ruined but reconstructible
- recoverable through archives
- symbolically important but physically lost
- too dangerous to restore
- intentionally preserved as ruins

Some war-damaged sites are rebuilt exactly.

Others are left partially ruined as memorials.

---

## 8. Monumental reconstruction begins slowly

The programme immediately encounters problems.

Rome lacks:
- enough skilled stoneworkers
- detailed records for many structures
- money
- stable foundations
- industrial materials
- agreement over which version of a building should be restored

Many monuments were rebuilt several times across history.

Restorers debate whether to reproduce:
- the original structure
- its most famous form
- its final pre-destruction form
- a composite of several eras

Rome eventually adopts a principle:

> Restoration must reveal history, not erase it.

A rebuilt structure may display several chronological layers rather than pretending only one period existed.

---

## 9. The Biosphere Restoration Programme

In **117,176**, the Republic establishes the **Great Terran Restoration**.

Its stated goal is to recover as much of Terra’s biosphere from before the Second Breaking as possible.

Scientists know complete restoration is impossible.

Many species depended on ecosystems that no longer exist.

Some genetic records are incomplete.

Some embryos are no longer viable.

Nevertheless, the programme begins searching for:
- DNA archives
- seed vaults
- frozen embryos
- microbial cultures
- tissue banks
- ecological records
- preserved reproductive cells
- artificial womb designs
- extinct-species husbandry manuals

Search teams explore:
- deep shelters
- abandoned mountain vaults
- drowned cities
- polar archives
- former Mandate research bunkers
- ancient orbital data records
- lunar vault catalogues

The programme quickly discovers that many of the best-preserved biological archives are believed to be on Luna.

This becomes one of the strongest arguments for returning there.

---

## 10. The Great Libraries Programme

In **117,178**, Rome approves the **Network of Perpetual Libraries**.

These are not ordinary libraries.

They are reinforced underground complexes designed to survive:
- asteroid impacts
- war
- fire
- flood
- political collapse
- epidemic
- industrial failure
- renewed IRF strengthening

At least one major library is planned for every large geographic region of the Republic.

Each is intended to contain knowledge in multiple forms:
- printed books
- engraved metal plates
- ceramic tablets
- photographic microtext
- magnetic storage
- optical storage
- electromechanical records
- biological data samples
- mathematical memory systems
- oral-recitation traditions

The same essential knowledge is duplicated across multiple sites.

No single medium is trusted.

No single language is trusted.

Although Latin Nova is primary, the libraries preserve:
- provincial languages
- ancient languages
- technical notation
- visual instructions
- universal mathematical descriptions

Construction begins during this century, but none approaches completion.

Each library is conceived as a project lasting centuries.

---

## 11. The philosophy of permanent survival

These programmes become part of a wider Roman doctrine called **Perpetuitas**.

Its central idea is that the Republic must never again depend entirely on one:
- political system
- energy source
- archive
- language
- city
- technology
- ecosystem
- military structure

Every critical institution must be:
- duplicated
- geographically dispersed
- understandable at multiple technological levels
- recoverable after collapse

The Republic begins planning not in decades, but in millennia.

This cultural shift strongly differentiates postwar Rome from the expansionist Republic that entered the Unification War.

---

## 12. Tertius

Tertius continues approaching Terra.

Its orbit is monitored continuously by:
- Roman observatories
- Atlantic League stations
- Martian astronomers
- independent states
- a small number of surviving orbital instruments

By **117,169**, the remaining uncertainty becomes negligible.

Tertius will not strike Terra.

It passes through the inner Solar System in **117,173**.

Its closest approach is several million kilometres from Terra.

It is visible through powerful telescopes but never becomes a dramatic naked-eye object.

The passage still causes mass public gatherings, religious ceremonies and emergency vigils.

No measurable IRF change occurs.

Spectral analysis confirms that Tertius does not contain significant field-memory material.

After passing Terra, it is redirected slightly by planetary gravity and continues into a long eccentric orbit.

It may return after many centuries, but calculations show no known future impact risk.

The asteroid scare ends.

The planetary-defence programme remains.

---

## 13. The first postwar economic crisis

Reconstruction produces rapid economic growth.

It also creates speculation.

Companies receive contracts for:
- railways
- libraries
- monument restoration
- biosphere recovery
- housing
- asteroid defence

Investors assume Roman reconstruction spending will expand forever.

By **117,181**, industrial and land prices become detached from real production.

The bubble collapses in **117,183**.

Several large banks fail.

Construction projects halt.

Unemployment rises.

Former Mandate provinces experience renewed unrest.

Rome responds by:
- guaranteeing household savings
- nationalising essential rail and water companies
- suspending some debt payments
- prosecuting fraudulent contractors
- creating public reconstruction employment

The crisis lasts five years.

It slows recovery but does not reverse it.

It also increases public support for stronger financial regulation.

---

## 14. Terra around year 117,185

By the mid-1180s, Terra’s total population has begun growing again.

Approximate population:

**3.8–4.0 billion.**

Roman population:

**2.5–2.7 billion.**

Living standards are improving, but unevenly.

In Roman core cities, people increasingly have access to:
- reliable electricity
- piped water
- antibiotics
- rail transport
- radio communication
- public schools
- manufactured household goods
- basic refrigeration

In recently annexed regions, many communities still face:
- damaged housing
- poor sanitation
- mine contamination
- unexploded weapons
- food insecurity
- weak courts
- mistrust of Roman officials

The Republic deliberately spends more per person in poorer provinces than in wealthy ones.

Core regions complain.

The government replies that unequal reconstruction would recreate the conditions that caused the war.

---

## 15. Expansion resumes

Rome does not launch wars of conquest.

Its expansion continues through accession.

Several poor independent states face:
- debt
- environmental collapse
- weak institutions
- food insecurity
- insurgency
- failing infrastructure

They observe that former integration provinces, though still poorer than the Roman core, are recovering faster than comparable independent states.

Rome offers membership under the new constitution.

Unlike the old integration treaties, new accessions receive:
- immediate Roman citizenship
- representation in national institutions
- equal legal status
- national investment
- no separate integration debt system
- no hereditary local authority

In return, they must accept:
- the national constitution
- Latin Nova
- abolition of sovereign armies
- Roman law
- taxation
- national standards
- one optional secondary provincial language

Some states accept readily.

Others hold bitter referenda.

A few governments sign despite local opposition, producing small rebellions.

Rome’s borders expand gradually.

---

## 16. The Atlantic League stagnates

The Atlantic League avoids collapse but loses dynamism.

Its problems include:
- wartime debt
- declining shipping dominance
- damaged ports
- demographic ageing
- internal tariff disputes
- resentment toward commercial elites
- competition from Roman continental infrastructure

The League remains wealthy in certain cities.

But it lacks Rome’s large internal reconstruction market.

It also fails to agree on a common postwar political direction.

Some members want closer cooperation with Rome.

Others want strict containment.

Others favour a return to commercial isolation.

The result is stagnation rather than renewal.

---

## 17. Secessions from the League

Between **117,184 and 117,214**, several smaller League members withdraw successfully.

The League protests but does not use large-scale force.

The scars of the Unification War make military suppression politically unacceptable.

Most seceding states become neutral commercial republics.

One, the **Republic of Caledon**, formally applies to join Rome in **117,207**.

Caledon is culturally close to several Roman provinces and economically dependent on Roman trade.

Its population votes strongly in favour of accession.

Rome rejects the application.

The refusal is respectful but firm.

The Senate explains that accepting Caledon would:
- humiliate the Atlantic League
- destabilise its remaining members
- encourage further defections
- create the appearance of encirclement
- risk a new geopolitical confrontation

Rome offers instead:
- free-trade access
- defence consultation
- educational exchange
- Roman investment
- dual residency rights

Caledon remains independent.

The League is surprised by Roman restraint.

Relations improve slightly.

---

## 18. No new cold war

Rome could probably defeat the weakened League in a future conflict.

Roman military planners know this.

They also calculate that victory would still cost millions of lives, destroy trade and damage reconstruction for generations.

No major political faction wants such a war.

Rome deliberately avoids:
- military encirclement
- aggressive naval expansion
- support for League separatists
- propaganda calling for immediate planetary annexation

The League likewise reduces direct anti-Roman activity.

The two blocs remain rivals, but their rivalry is regulated and limited.

They cooperate on:
- asteroid tracking
- navigation standards
- disease control
- maritime rescue
- orbital debris monitoring

There is no warm friendship.

There is also no new cold war.

---

## 19. The return to orbit

Rome’s lunar programme restarts cautiously.

The first goal is not Luna.

It is safe access to orbit.

The debris problem is severe.

Decades of war left:
- shattered satellites
- failed rocket stages
- explosive fragments
- abandoned military platforms
- unknown orbital mines
- untracked metal clouds

The Republic establishes the **Orbital Cartography Service**.

It uses:
- ground radar
- optical tracking
- surviving satellites
- mathematical orbit prediction
- Atlantic League data
- ancient archive models

The first orbital maps are incomplete.

Every launch remains dangerous.

---

## 20. The Debris Clearance Era

Between **117,184 and 117,205**, Rome develops several methods to reduce orbital risk.

These include:
- sacrificial shield satellites
- low-energy debris tugs
- nets
- magnetic collectors
- controlled collision vehicles
- atmospheric-drag sails
- ground-directed orbit alteration
- carefully timed launch corridors

Large debris cannot always be removed.

Instead, Rome maps safe windows through the orbital environment.

Launches occur only during narrow periods when known objects are elsewhere.

Several missions still fail.

In **117,191**, a debris impact destroys an orbital laboratory and kills fourteen astronauts.

The disaster nearly ends the programme.

Public support survives because the mission had been studying planetary defence.

---

## 21. The first Roman postwar crewed orbit

In **117,198**, Rome launches a three-person crew into stable orbit.

The mission lasts five days.

Its objectives are:
- test life support
- validate debris tracking
- photograph Luna
- inspect an old communications satellite
- measure the IRF above Terra

The mission returns safely.

It becomes the first major technological triumph of the reconstructed Republic.

Unlike pre-war missions, it is presented not as a victory over rival powers but as a shared Roman achievement.

Atlantic League observers are invited to the launch.

Mars receives a compressed radio report.

---

## 22. The lunar programme

After the successful orbital mission, Rome formally creates **Iter Lunae II**.

The programme has four purposes:

1. recover biological archives;
2. access ancient scientific records;
3. inspect sealed lunar habitats;
4. establish whether permanent low-energy human presence can return.

The mission is not designed initially to colonise Luna.

The IRF there remains too strong, and the environment remains deadly.

The first expedition will be archaeological, scientific and biological.

---

## 23. Obstacles to reaching Luna

Rome faces enormous difficulties.

## Orbital debris

The crew must pass through dangerous orbital zones.

## IRF instability

Engines behave differently as the vehicle moves away from Terra’s weaker cavity.

## Navigation

Electronic systems are still bulky and unreliable.

## Life support

The journey requires several days of closed-system operation.

## Lunar landing

No modern landing sites exist.

Ancient surfaces may be unstable or contaminated.

## Return

The vehicle must launch from Luna under stronger IRF conditions than near the Wound.

## Politics

Some citizens oppose spending resources on Luna while poorer provinces remain underdeveloped.

The programme survives because the biosphere and archive missions are considered part of planetary survival.

---

## 24. First unmanned return to Luna

In **117,204**, Rome lands an uncrewed probe on Luna.

It touches down several kilometres from an ancient archive entrance.

The landing is rough but successful.

The probe transmits:
- images of preserved structures
- radiation readings
- local IRF data
- surface composition
- evidence that some underground chambers may remain sealed

A drilling attempt fails.

The probe survives for eighty-three days.

Its final images show a partially buried access structure marked with symbols from the Great Release.

The public reaction is enormous.

---

## 25. The First Lunar Return

Rome launches the first crewed lunar expedition in **117,216**.

The mission is called **Redditus I**—The Return.

The crew consists of six people:
- two pilots
- an engineer
- a physician
- an archivist
- a biosystems specialist

The spacecraft uses:
- chemical propulsion
- mechanically redundant guidance
- thick debris shielding
- multiple independent life-support loops
- optical navigation
- low-energy emergency systems

The journey nearly fails twice.

A debris fragment damages an external radiator in Terra orbit.

Later, an engine suffers unstable thrust during lunar descent.

The crew lands several kilometres from the intended site.

They become the first humans to stand on Luna since the Closing Sky.

---

## 26. What they find

Luna is silent.

The surface is covered with ruins, dust and abandoned machinery.

The crew enters a partially sealed underground complex.

Inside they find:
- frozen corridors
- preserved equipment
- collapsed agricultural chambers
- human remains
- ceramic data vaults
- mechanical archive systems
- sealed biological storage rooms

Most delicate electronics are dead.

Some passive media remain readable.

The most important discovery is a set of cryogenic genetic repositories.

Many samples have degraded.

Some remain potentially recoverable.

The crew retrieves:
- DNA libraries
- seed records
- embryo catalogues
- tissue samples
- ecological databases
- lunar historical archives
- maps of deeper vaults

They remain on Luna for nineteen days.

The return launch succeeds narrowly.

A propulsion fluctuation forces the crew to abandon some collected material to reduce mass.

All six return alive.

---

## 27. The meaning of the lunar landing

The landing transforms Roman society.

For the first time in thousands of years, humans have returned to another world.

The achievement is not treated primarily as imperial triumph.

It is remembered as the reconnection of two broken parts of humanity’s history.

The mission strengthens:
- the Biosphere Restoration Programme
- the Great Libraries
- archive research
- planetary defence
- Mars communication
- public faith in reconstruction

It also creates pressure for permanent lunar bases.

Rome refuses immediate colonisation.

The government argues that one successful mission does not make Luna safe.

---

## 28. Further lunar missions

By year 117,260, Rome has completed seven crewed lunar missions.

Two suffer serious accidents.

One loses its lander and returns without landing.

No crew is permanently stranded.

The missions establish a small temporary surface station used only during visits.

They recover:
- additional genetic samples
- ancient maps
- medical records
- mechanical components
- dormant data cores
- historical recordings
- samples of long-frozen lunar ecosystems

A permanent settlement is still considered premature.

The IRF on Luna remains too strong for reliable large-scale industry.

---

## 29. Biosphere restoration results

The lunar archives greatly expand the Great Terran Restoration.

Scientists recover records for thousands of lost organisms.

Actual restoration is far more difficult.

Successful early recoveries include:
- several crop varieties
- extinct pollinating insects
- freshwater fish lineages
- rare tree populations
- microbial soil communities
- a few small mammals
- preserved coral-associated organisms

Large animals are harder.

Even when embryos survive, suitable habitats may not.

Rome establishes protected reconstruction reserves.

Some restored species adapt successfully.

Others die out again.

Scientists increasingly understand that restoration cannot mean recreating the old biosphere exactly.

It means recovering as much genetic and ecological diversity as Terra can now support.

---

## 30. The monument programme expands

Economic recovery allows larger projects.

By year 117,220, restoration is underway in hundreds of cities.

Rome rebuilds:
- civic forums
- bridges
- temples
- observatories
- libraries
- ancient theatres
- industrial halls
- memorial structures

Former Mandate capitals receive major restoration funding.

This is deliberate.

Rome wants conquered populations to see their histories preserved rather than erased.

The Republic’s message is:

> Roman unity does not require historical uniformity.

Some critics dismiss the programme as expensive symbolism.

Supporters argue that a unified civilisation needs shared access to all of its pasts.

---

## 31. The Great Libraries take shape

The first major underground library complex opens partially in **117,223**.

It is not complete.

Its completed sections already contain:
- millions of printed volumes
- engraved scientific summaries
- maps
- legal codes
- biological archives
- industrial designs
- oral-history recordings
- language collections
- astronomical data

By year 117,260, nine major library sites are under construction.

Three are partially operational.

None yet contains the full intended archive.

Each site is designed to survive independently if all others are lost.

---

## 32. A political setback: the Standardisation Riots

The Republic’s national standards improve administration but create resistance.

In several provinces, communities object to:
- compulsory Latin education
- replacement of local measures
- national school curricula
- abolition of traditional legal customs
- standardised land registration

The most serious unrest occurs between **117,224 and 117,227**.

Demonstrations become riots.

Local officials refuse national orders.

A few police units defect to protesters.

Rome initially responds too harshly, causing deaths and further unrest.

The Senate later changes policy.

It maintains national standards for:
- law
- engineering
- medicine
- trade
- administration

But allows traditional systems to survive in:
- festivals
- local crafts
- cultural education
- informal commerce
- religious practice

The crisis demonstrates that standardisation without cultural tact can appear indistinguishable from erasure.

---

## 33. Economic prosperity

After the crisis of the 1180s and the slower growth of the early century, the Roman economy expands strongly from roughly **117,200 onward**.

Growth is driven by:
- reconstruction
- continental trade
- standard rail systems
- public health
- electrification
- lunar technology
- agricultural restoration
- reduced military spending
- development in poor provinces

Formerly marginal regions become industrial centres.

The Republic’s economy becomes less concentrated around its old western core.

This is one of the Equalisation Programme’s greatest successes.

---

## 34. Terra around year 117,210

Approximate total Terra population:

**4.1–4.4 billion.**

Roman population:

**2.8–3.1 billion.**

Average living standards now exceed their immediate pre-war levels.

In the most developed Roman regions, citizens commonly have:
- electricity
- reliable sanitation
- radio
- rail access
- antibiotics
- public clinics
- refrigeration
- mechanised transport
- standard education

In poorer provinces, life remains more rural and physically demanding.

But famine is much rarer.

Migration pressure declines sharply.

The majority of movement within Rome is now economic choice rather than desperate flight.

---

## 35. Social cohesion

Roman identity grows, but it does not replace regional identities.

A typical citizen may identify as:
- Roman nationally
- provincial administratively
- local culturally
- linguistic or religious by heritage

Military service is no longer the main source of shared identity.

Common institutions become more important:
- schools
- elections
- railways
- national law
- disaster relief
- Latin Nova
- public-health systems

Citizens in former Mandate regions initially use “Roman” cautiously.

By the middle of the century, younger generations increasingly treat it as a normal civic identity.

---

## 36. Minor conflicts

No large-scale wars occur.

Local conflicts continue.

They include:
- border disputes
- separatist insurgencies
- land conflicts
- former aristocratic revolts
- criminal control of wartime weapon stocks
- clashes over water
- conflicts around new industrial zones

Rome usually responds through police, courts and negotiation rather than mass armies.

The most serious postwar conflict lasts four years in a mountainous former Mandate province.

It ends through a combination of amnesty, infrastructure investment and targeted military operations.

Casualties number in the tens of thousands, not millions.

---

## 37. The Atlantic League by mid-century

The League’s population remains substantial, but economic growth is weak.

Some major ports prosper.

Interior member states stagnate.

The League’s political institutions become more defensive and less ambitious.

It survives because its members still value:
- maritime access
- commercial law
- financial expertise
- independence from Rome

But it no longer presents a convincing alternative model for organising Terra.

Its most successful reform is a partial democratisation of its commercial councils.

This reduces public anger but does not produce renewed expansion.

---

## 38. Relations between Rome and the League

Rome and the League gradually develop a restrained partnership.

They cooperate on:
- asteroid observation
- lunar navigation
- orbital debris
- maritime safety
- epidemic response
- scientific standards

Rome allows League scientists to participate in some lunar missions.

The League grants Roman ships easier access to its ports.

The major unresolved issues are:
- trade policy
- seceded League states
- naval limits
- access to restored lunar knowledge
- Roman expansion into poor neutral states

Neither side wants conflict.

Both sides maintain military contingency plans.

---

## 39. Mars during the century

Mars remains alive.

Its population grows slowly from approximately **130,000–200,000** in 117,160 to roughly **180,000–260,000** by 117,260.

This is the first sustained Martian population growth in many millennia.

## IRF

Mars remains under approximately **86–91%** intensity.

The decline is perceptible scientifically but offers little immediate technological relief.

## Environment

The planet remains unbreathable.

However:
- enclosed ecosystems are stable
- microbial life spreads slowly outside habitats
- subsurface reservoirs remain productive
- geothermal settlements expand cautiously
- some sealed valleys support permanent lakes

## Daily life

Martians still devote mandatory time to:
- maintenance
- ecological monitoring
- water management
- emergency drills

Food remains repetitive but no longer precarious.

Some settlements produce luxury foods for ceremonial use.

## Society

The Martian Covenant develops stronger coordinating institutions.

It still does not become a single state.

It creates:
- shared ecological law
- common communication standards
- inter-settlement medical exchange
- a planetary archive council

## Outlook

Mars’s long-term survival now appears likely.

Its population is small, but its ecosystems are mature enough to persist until the IRF weakens further.

---

## 40. Roman–Martian relations

The lunar programme improves communications with Mars.

Lunar relay equipment provides better transmission geometry.

Rome begins receiving more complete Martian messages.

The two worlds exchange:
- astronomical observations
- ecological techniques
- medical knowledge
- history
- engineering designs

Rome cannot yet send a mission to Mars.

The energy requirements remain too high, especially because IRF intensity increases along the route.

Mars becomes a recognised partner rather than a distant survivor.

---

## 41. Europa during the century

Europa’s population is estimated at **140,000–320,000** by 117,260.

The uncertainty remains large.

## IRF

Europa remains at approximately **98–100%** intensity.

No meaningful technological release reaches it.

## Ecology

Its hydrothermal ecosystems continue maturing.

Some communities cultivate entire artificial reef systems around vent regions.

Others migrate when geological activity shifts.

## Society

Europan groups increasingly share a common identity through rare long-distance acoustic and radio communication.

They remain politically divided into many communities.

No unified Europan government forms.

## Daily life

Most life remains aquatic or semi-aquatic.

Dry environments are reserved for:
- archives
- medicine
- childhood care
- specialised manufacturing

## Outlook

Europa appears stable but vulnerable to geology.

Its population is unlikely to grow rapidly.

Its survival no longer seems doubtful.

---

## 42. Titan during the century

Titan’s population rises modestly to approximately **15–21 million**.

## IRF

The local field remains effectively at **100%**.

Titan sees no technological release.

## Environment

Underground biospheres continue expanding.

Some separate cavern systems connect through controlled tunnels.

The surface remains almost unchanged:
- methane rain
- hydrocarbon lakes
- frozen dunes
- preserved industrial ruins

## Daily life

Heat remains the central economic unit.

Citizens receive thermal and water allocations.

Most work in:
- ecology
- heat management
- maintenance
- chemistry
- archive preservation

## Society

Titan remains divided into cavern republics and thermal federations.

Some hereditary engineering houses survive, though their political authority slowly weakens.

## Outlook

Titan is extremely stable.

It has little growth potential but low extinction risk.

It remains the largest offworld human population.

---

## 43. The extrasolar colony receives a name

The surviving naturally habitable extrasolar colony is known to its inhabitants as **Novaterra**.

The name was chosen by the original colonists before the IRF overtook the system.

It is not an untouched living world.

When humans arrived, Novaterra was completely barren.

It possessed:
- liquid water
- stable oceans
- suitable atmospheric pressure
- oxygen-poor but modifiable air
- Earthlike gravity
- a day approximately twenty-five hours long
- moderate seasonal cycles
- mineral-rich continents
- no native life

Every organism now living there descends from species transported from Terra.

---

## 44. The creation of Novaterra’s biosphere

The colonists initially survived in sealed settlements.

They introduced life in stages:

1. microbes and algae;
2. soil-forming bacteria;
3. lichens and mosses;
4. hardy grasses;
5. insects;
6. small freshwater organisms;
7. shrubs and trees;
8. larger animals in controlled regions.

The process took thousands of years.

For centuries, most of the planet remained barren.

Even after local ecosystems became stable, they did not spread evenly.

Mountains, deserts, cold regions and chemically unsuitable soils resisted colonisation.

By 117,260, Novaterra possesses a substantial but incomplete biosphere.

Large portions of its temperate continents are green.

Other regions remain:
- bare rock
- microbial crust
- shallow algal wetlands
- sterile desert
- partially engineered soil

The planet is alive because humans made it alive.

---

## 45. Novaterran ecology

All life is Terran-derived.

However, thousands of years of isolation have produced evolutionary divergence.

Common features include:
- plants adapted to the planet’s slightly different light
- insects with altered seasonal cycles
- fish adapted to mineral-rich waters
- soil microbes unlike any current Terran strain
- forests with simplified food webs
- animals with fewer natural predators

Because there was no native life, Novaterra’s ecosystems are less complex than Terra’s.

They are also easier to destabilise.

Some regions experience massive population swings when introduced species escape control.

Ecological management is one of the planet’s central sciences.

---

## 46. Population of Novaterra

By year 117,260, Novaterra contains approximately **45–70 million people**.

Population growth is limited by the slow expansion of usable ecosystems.

Large cities are rare.

Most inhabitants live in:
- river valleys
- coastal plains
- older terraformed basins
- forest belts
- agricultural corridors

The planet has abundant physical space but limited biologically mature land.

Population policy therefore remains cautious.

---

## 47. Novaterra’s political structure

Novaterra is not politically unified.

It contains **nine major states** and dozens of smaller territories.

The major states include:
- agricultural republics
- river federations
- archive city-states
- communal ecological unions
- hereditary-founded polities that later democratised
- technical councils descended from colonial management authorities

The states cooperate through the **Novaterran Concord**.

The Concord manages:
- atmospheric monitoring
- biosphere protection
- quarantine
- interregional water systems
- shared archives
- conflict mediation
- transport standards

It is not a world government.

Its authority is strongest in ecological matters.

Wars are rare because large-scale destruction could damage the fragile biosphere.

Local conflicts still occur over water, forest access and settlement rights.

---

## 48. Novaterran languages

Novaterra has approximately **fourteen major living languages** and hundreds of smaller dialects.

Most descend from languages spoken aboard the original colony fleets.

One shared language, **Common Transit**, developed from technical and operational speech used during the early settlement period.

It is used for:
- intergovernmental communication
- science
- navigation
- ecological management
- Concord law

No one believes it is still identical to its ancient form.

Unlike Rome, Novaterra never created a formal language-preservation programme.

Its languages drifted naturally.

Ancient Solar languages survive mainly in archives and religious or scholarly use.

---

## 49. Daily life on Novaterra

Daily life varies by region.

In established green zones, people live in:
- agricultural towns
- river cities
- forest settlements
- coastal ports

They use:
- wind power
- water power
- low-voltage electricity
- mechanical rail
- sailing vessels
- electromechanical computation
- biological medicine
- sophisticated ecological monitoring

Industrial capability remains impossible under full IRF intensity.

People possess advanced scientific knowledge but limited machinery.

Many occupations centre on biosphere work:
- soil building
- water management
- seed distribution
- species control
- forest expansion
- atmospheric study

A child may spend part of every school year helping establish soil or monitor new ecosystems.

---

## 50. Novaterran well-being

In mature regions, living standards resemble stable pre-industrial Terra at its best.

People have:
- good sanitation
- high literacy
- basic medicine
- secure food
- strong communities
- relatively low warfare

Life expectancy ranges from approximately **65 to 85 years**.

In frontier regions, life is harsher.

Settlers face:
- crop failure
- thin soils
- unpredictable ecosystems
- isolation
- limited medicine
- dangerous weather

Novaterra’s civilisation is not wealthy.

It is patient.

Its central historical project is not industrialisation.

It is turning a barren planet into a living one.

---

## 51. Novaterra’s knowledge of Sol

Novaterrans preserve records of Terra, Luna, Mars and the Closing Sky.

They do not know the current condition of the Solar System.

Many scholars assume Terra still supports humans because of its natural biosphere.

Others believe repeated catastrophe may have destroyed civilisation there.

Sol appears in Novaterran culture as:
- ancestral home
- lost centre
- cautionary history
- sacred origin
- possible surviving civilisation

No practical communication exists.

The IRF between the systems remains too strong, and Novaterra lacks high-power transmitters.

---

## 52. The endless colony ship

The surviving ship continues beyond the IRF frontier.

Its population is estimated in the high hundreds of thousands.

The vessel remains industrial because it has not yet been overtaken by the field.

Its society becomes increasingly self-contained.

During this century:
- propulsion is reduced to conserve resources
- artificial ecosystems are expanded
- political disputes grow over whether to continue fleeing
- a new generation questions the original mission
- astronomers detect subtle anomalies in the shape of the distant IRF frontier

They do not yet understand that the field is thinning behind them.

The first true recognition will occur much later.

---

## 53. Terra’s later-century setback: the Restoration Plague

In **117,231**, a disease emerges in several restored wetland regions.

It is linked not to an ancient recovered organism, but to interactions between:
- revived microbial strains
- modern post-impact species
- changed water chemistry

The illness spreads through river systems.

It kills several million people over six years.

The Biosphere Restoration Programme is blamed.

Public confidence collapses.

Some provinces destroy restoration reserves.

Rome suspends several reintroduction projects.

A later investigation finds that the disaster resulted from inadequate ecological quarantine rather than the restored species alone.

New rules require:
- multigenerational testing
- isolated trial ecosystems
- genetic monitoring
- regional consent
- emergency containment plans

The programme survives, but becomes far more cautious.

---

## 54. Political life late in the century

Roman democracy remains healthy, though contentious.

Major political blocs include:
- conservative preservationists
- social equalisation parties
- technological restorationists
- provincial cultural parties
- ecological restoration movements
- commercial liberals
- lunar expansion advocates

Governments change through elections.

No faction permanently dominates.

The constitutional court repeatedly limits executive authority.

The army remains under civilian control.

Political violence exists, but it is local rather than systemic.

The Republic has become more conservative in culture and planning, but not less democratic.

## 55. Terra around year 117,235

Terra’s population reaches approximately **4.5–4.8 billion**.

Rome contains roughly **3.2–3.5 billion**.

Living standards continue rising.

Urban citizens increasingly enjoy:
- dependable power
- mass transit
- clean water
- radio and wired information networks
- advanced surgery
- broader pharmaceutical access
- mechanised work
- public libraries
- regular elections

Rural regions improve through:
- irrigation
- rail access
- agricultural machinery
- clinics
- electrification
- soil restoration

Regional inequality remains substantial, but the gap is narrowing.

## 56. The shape of Roman expansion by the end of the century

Rome adds several territories through peaceful accession.

Most are poor states seeking:
- food security
- infrastructure
- legal stability
- access to Roman markets
- environmental recovery

Rome does not annex wealthy Atlantic League members.

It does not force neutral states to join.

Its expansion is slow enough to avoid triggering a new global coalition.

By 117,260, the Republic governs perhaps **75–80% of Terra’s population**, though much less than that proportion of the planet’s surface.

Large uninhabited and sparsely populated regions remain outside practical state control.

## 57. Terra in year 117,260

## Population

Approximately **4.8–5.1 billion**.

## Roman population

Approximately **3.6–4.0 billion**.

## Environment

Terra is visibly recovering from the war.

- forests return to abandoned battlefields
- rivers are being cleaned
- contaminated zones remain
- restored species inhabit protected reserves
- agriculture is more productive
- urban pollution is better regulated

## Technology

At roughly **58.5% IRF intensity**, Terra possesses:
- mature regional electrical grids
- powerful industrial chemistry
- widespread aviation
- crewed orbital flight
- repeated lunar missions
- sophisticated electromechanical computing
- mass-produced medicine
- continental rail systems
- advanced mechanical automation

Still constrained:
- semiconductor-scale electronics
- nuclear power
- fusion
- cheap orbital launch
- interplanetary travel beyond Luna
- high-density global computing
- fully industrial artificial intelligence

## Living standards

The best Roman regions approach moderate industrial-era quality of life.

The poorest remain materially closer to advanced pre-industrial societies.

## Social cohesion

Much stronger than in 117,160.

War identities are fading.

Shared citizenship, law, language and infrastructure increasingly bind the Republic.

---

## 58. The Republic’s century-long transformation

Rome begins the century as a victorious but exhausted military state.

It ends it as a prosperous, conservative-democratic civilisation committed to:
- permanent language
- permanent archives
- permanent constitutional continuity
- biosphere restoration
- regional equality
- cultural preservation
- planetary defence
- eventual return to the wider Solar System

None of its great projects is close to completion.

The libraries are incomplete.

Most monuments remain ruined.

The old biosphere cannot be fully restored.

Luna has only been visited, not resettled.

Many provinces remain poor.

The Atlantic League remains separate.

Yet the direction is clear.

Rome no longer thinks merely in terms of winning the next election, surviving the next war or reaching the next technological threshold.

It plans for citizens who may live ten thousand years later.

## 59. The final image of year 117,260

In Rome, a child studies Permanent Latin from a textbook printed according to rules intended never to change.

In a former Mandate province, workers rebuild a ruined observatory using standard Roman measurements.

In an underground library, ceramic tablets are sealed behind pressure doors beside magnetic archives and printed books.

On a protected plain, the first healthy population of a restored animal species is released into the wild.

Above Terra, Roman crews navigate between mapped fields of ancient orbital debris.

On Luna, a temporary station stands beside frozen ruins older than entire civilisations.

On Mars, Covenant astronomers receive a Roman transmission confirming another safe lunar return.

On Europa, human descendants move between warm vents beneath kilometres of ice.

On Titan, cavern forests grow beneath a world of methane rain.

On Novaterra, settlers plant forests into soil that did not exist before humanity arrived.

And far beyond the IRF frontier, a ship continues through the dark, still fleeing a field whose heart is already fading.
