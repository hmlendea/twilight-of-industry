  The next 140 years are remembered less for a single dramatic event than for a fundamental change in Roman ambition.

  During the previous century, the Roman Republic concentrated on survival, reconstruction and constitutional stability. Now it begins asking a more difficult question:

  > If the Republic can endure, what kind of civilisation should it become?

  Rome’s answer is increasingly utopian.

  It seeks not only to eliminate famine and extreme poverty, but to bring every province toward comparable living standards. It wants citizens to be free to move, yet also wants to make migration unnecessary by ensuring that no region is abandoned economically. It wants to preserve the past, restore the biosphere, recover the lost technology of the Industrial Age and maintain enough resilience to survive another collapse.

  It does not fully achieve these goals by year 117,400.

  But it moves far enough that many previously independent states begin regarding Roman-led civilisation as the default future of Terra.

# 117260-117400 — The Age of Equalisation

## The IRF during this period

The background decline remains smooth.

Terra’s global average falls from approximately:

- 58.5% in 117,260
- 58.0% in 117,310
- 57.5% in 117,360
- 57.1% in 117,400

The next periodic burst is still centuries away, in year **118,110**.

Approximate conditions in 117,400 are:

| Region | Approximate IRF intensity |
| --- | --- |
| Wound of Return | 48–51% |
| Terra average | 57.1% |
| Near-Terran orbit | 59–62% |
| Luna | 64–69% |
| Mars | 83–89% |
| Asteroid belt | 91–96% |
| Europa/Jupiter | 97–100% |
| Titan/Saturn | ~100% |
| Novaterra and other overtaken systems | 100% |
| Beyond the expanding IRF frontier | 0% |

The decline is still too slow to produce a sudden technological revolution.

It does, however, make machinery slightly more compact, reliable and powerful with every generation.

---

## 1. Rome in year 117,260

At the beginning of the period, the Roman Republic governs roughly **3.6–4.0 billion people**.

Terra as a whole contains approximately **4.8–5.1 billion**.

Rome controls most of the world’s population, industrial capacity, scientific institutions and long-distance infrastructure. Its borders continue expanding through accession rather than conquest.

The Atlantic League remains the largest independent bloc, but it is increasingly overshadowed economically.

Several smaller states still believe they can maintain complete independence indefinitely.

Others already conduct most of their trade, science and infrastructure planning according to Roman standards.

Rome has not formally become the world government.

In practice, it is beginning to perform many of the functions of one.

---

## 2. The Equal Footing doctrine

In **117,268**, the Senate adopts the **Doctrine of Equal Footing**.

It states that every Roman province should eventually possess comparable access to:

- food security
- clean water
- electricity
- healthcare
- education
- transportation
- communications
- disaster protection
- legal services
- cultural institutions

The doctrine does not require every region to possess identical industries or incomes.

A remote agricultural province need not resemble an industrial metropolis.

But a child born there should not have dramatically worse prospects because of geography.

The doctrine becomes the moral basis for the next century of Roman economic policy.

---

## 3. Measuring inequality

Rome creates a national statistical service to compare provincial conditions.

It tracks:

- income
- life expectancy
- infant mortality
- literacy
- energy access
- travel time to hospitals
- housing quality
- water security
- food costs
- environmental stability
- employment
- disaster vulnerability

The results are politically explosive.

The wealthiest provinces enjoy living standards approaching the mature Industrial Age.

The poorest still resemble advanced pre-industrial societies.

Life expectancy varies by more than twenty years between regions.

Some provincial representatives accuse the government of publishing humiliating data.

Others argue that inequality cannot be corrected if it remains hidden.

---

## 4. The Universal Civic Dividend

Rome’s most ambitious social proposal emerges in **117,276**.

The **Universal Civic Dividend** is intended to provide every Roman citizen with a minimum unconditional income.

Its supporters describe it as:

- a guarantee of civic independence
- protection against automation
- compensation for national ownership of resources
- a tool against regional poverty
- a means of reducing bureaucratic welfare systems

Its opponents argue that the Republic cannot afford it.

The government initially tests the system in:

- heavily automated industrial cities
- former mining regions
- depopulated rural provinces
- communities affected by ecological restoration restrictions

The trials show positive results:

- extreme poverty declines
- small businesses increase
- food insecurity falls
- workers become less dependent on abusive employers
- migration motivated solely by desperation decreases

But costs are enormous.

---

## 5. A partial universal income

Rome does not achieve a generous full universal basic income during this timeframe.

Instead, it gradually creates a **universal civic floor**.

By year **117,332**, every citizen is entitled to a modest national payment sufficient to cover part—but not all—of basic living costs.

The payment is supplemented by guaranteed access to:

- staple food support
- emergency housing
- basic healthcare
- public education
- local transportation concessions
- minimum electricity and water allocations

The civic floor prevents destitution.

It does not eliminate the need to work.

Wealthier provinces add their own supplements.

Poorer provinces depend more heavily on national funding.

By 117,400, Rome has achieved something approaching universal economic security, but not a fully work-optional income.

---

## 6. Funding the civic floor

The programme is financed through:

- progressive income taxes
- industrial automation levies
- resource royalties
- national land rents
- public corporations
- inheritance taxes
- tariffs on luxury goods
- returns from lunar salvage and patents
- taxes on large financial transfers

The inheritance tax is especially controversial.

Rome has already abolished hereditary political authority.

Now it seeks to prevent enormous hereditary economic dynasties from replacing the old aristocracies.

Families may still pass on property.

Very large inheritances are heavily taxed.

This strengthens the Republic’s conservative-democratic identity: private property remains protected, but no family is supposed to become a permanent ruling class.

---

## 7. Planned internal migration

Rome’s second great social project is the **Balanced Settlement Programme**.

The government encourages citizens to move from:

- overcrowded cities
- environmentally stressed regions
- expensive industrial centres

toward:

- depopulated former war zones
- newly restored agricultural regions
- underdeveloped provincial cities
- frontier reconstruction districts
- regions near new rail and energy corridors

Participation is officially voluntary.

Migrants receive:

- housing
- land leases
- civic grants
- employment guarantees
- transport
- tax reductions
- education support
- relocation assistance

The programme seeks to distribute population more evenly without repeating the forced relocations of the Mandate.

---

## 8. Successes and failures of population redistribution

The programme works best where migrants move to functioning towns with:

- rail access
- hospitals
- schools
- reliable water
- existing local populations

It fails where planners treat empty land as sufficient.

Several newly constructed settlements lack:

- local economic purpose
- cultural institutions
- climate adaptation
- community cohesion

Many residents leave after their subsidies expire.

The Republic learns that settlement cannot be engineered solely through maps and quotas.

By 117,400, internal population distribution is more balanced than in 117,260, but major cities remain attractive and densely populated.

Rome reduces dangerous overcrowding.

It does not create perfectly even settlement.

---

## 9. Provincial equalisation

The government invests heavily in historically poor regions.

Major projects include:

- continental water-transfer systems
- rural electrification
- standard-gauge railways
- medical colleges
- industrial parks
- public housing
- soil restoration
- flood control
- local archive centres
- regional universities

The poorest provinces experience the fastest percentage growth.

Some become manufacturing centres because land and labour remain cheaper than in the old Roman core.

Others specialise in:

- agriculture
- ecological restoration
- medicine
- education
- tourism
- historical preservation
- energy generation

By the end of the period, extreme regional inequality is significantly reduced.

Equal living standards are not fully achieved.

---

## 10. Rome’s slow expansion

Rome continues admitting new territories.

Accession is now governed by a formal process:

1. a state requests negotiations;
2. Roman commissions assess its institutions, debt and infrastructure;
3. a transition treaty is drafted;
4. citizens vote in a referendum;
5. national institutions approve admission;
6. the state becomes one or more ordinary Roman provinces.

New members immediately receive Roman citizenship.

They must accept:

- the constitution
- Latin Nova
- national law
- abolition of hereditary authority
- taxation
- disarmament of independent armed forces
- Roman standards

Several desperate poor states join because they see no credible alternative.

Their governments are near collapse.

Roman membership brings:

- food guarantees
- currency stability
- public investment
- debt restructuring
- access to the Roman market
- security

Accession remains costly for Rome, but the Republic can now absorb new territories without the exploitative integration treaties of the earlier era.

---

## 11. Foreign development partnerships

Rome also invests outside its borders.

The **External Association Programme**, founded in **117,286**, offers independent states:

- railway construction
- medical aid
- electrification
- disaster relief
- university partnerships
- access to Roman standards
- low-interest development loans
- trade privileges

Rome does not require immediate accession.

It does require transparency, labour protections and anti-corruption supervision.

The programme is partly humanitarian and partly strategic.

Associated states become economically tied to Rome.

Their professionals learn Latin Nova.

Their infrastructure connects to Roman networks.

Their laws gradually become compatible with Roman law.

Many remain formally independent while moving steadily into Rome’s orbit.

---

## 12. Independent states confront Roman gravity

By the early 117,300s, some independent governments realise that Rome is becoming more than a neighbouring power.

It controls:

- the principal planetary scientific institutions
- lunar access
- most high-level medical research
- common technical standards
- the largest internal market
- the dominant currency system
- much of global disaster response
- asteroid tracking
- communications infrastructure

A few political thinkers argue that independence is becoming symbolic.

Their states already rely on Rome for:

- trade
- medicine
- defence coordination
- scientific data
- transport standards

They conclude that full membership is inevitable.

Most independent populations reject this conclusion.

They still believe they can maintain sovereignty and comparable living standards.

For the time being, several succeed.

---

## 13. The Atlantic League’s long decline

The Atlantic League continues losing relative power.

Its economy does not collapse.

Major ports remain prosperous.

Its financial and maritime expertise remains valuable.

But capital increasingly flows toward Rome because Rome offers:

- faster growth
- larger markets
- public reconstruction projects
- political stability
- expanding consumer demand
- lunar technology
- industrial standardisation

League companies move headquarters into Roman cities.

Engineers migrate.

Banks open Roman subsidiaries.

The League becomes increasingly dependent on Roman growth while trying to preserve political independence.

---

## 14. More League secessions

Three additional League states withdraw between **117,291 and 117,347**.

The separations are peaceful but tense.

The League imposes temporary commercial restrictions.

Rome refuses to encourage the secessions openly.

The former members enter association agreements with Rome.

These agreements include:

- tariff reduction
- Roman infrastructure investment
- legal compatibility
- scientific exchange
- mutual disaster assistance

Two eventually request Roman accession.

Both applications are delayed indefinitely.

Rome still fears that accepting former League states would humiliate and destabilise the League.

Instead, the states become economically Roman-aligned while remaining legally independent.

---

## 15. Roman pacifism

The Republic becomes increasingly pacifist in its external policy.

This does not mean disarmament.

Rome maintains the strongest military on Terra.

It means political leaders consider armed conflict a failure of civilisation.

The military focuses on:

- territorial defence
- disaster response
- asteroid emergencies
- peacekeeping
- protection of global infrastructure
- suppression of piracy and trafficking

Rome prefers:

- economic influence
- standards
- investment
- diplomacy
- association treaties
- cultural prestige

Its critics call this peaceful imperialism.

Supporters call it integration without conquest.

---

## 16. The Preservation Century

The cultural programmes founded in the previous century expand dramatically.

The Institute of Perpetual Latin completes:

- a definitive grammar
- standard pronunciation archives
- regional teaching networks
- a permanent technical vocabulary system
- engraved linguistic reference sets stored in every Great Library

Latin Nova remains stable in formal use.

Informal spoken varieties continue diverging, but education repeatedly brings them back toward the standard.

By 117,400, a citizen can still understand recordings from 117,160 easily.

The preservation project is considered successful so far.

Its true test will require millennia.

---

## 17. The Great Works Registry

Rome completes the first comprehensive registry of monumental human structures within its territory.

It contains hundreds of thousands of sites.

These include:

- surviving ancient buildings
- partially buried cities
- industrial ruins
- Long Age observatories
- impact shelters
- war memorials
- early space facilities
- restored religious structures
- civic architecture

Only a fraction can be rebuilt.

Priority is given to sites that are:

- structurally endangered
- historically unique
- symbolically important
- useful for modern civic life

Restoration becomes a major profession.

Architects are trained in multiple historical styles and structural methods.

---

## 18. Rebuilt cities

Several major cities regain monumental centres.

They do not become exact replicas of earlier eras.

Instead, reconstruction integrates:

- preserved ruins
- modern transport
- public housing
- ecological corridors
- civic institutions
- historical architecture

Former Mandate cities receive particularly large investments.

Rome wants the eastern provinces to see themselves as co-owners of civilisation, not conquered margins.

Some of the Republic’s finest universities and museums are established in former Mandate capitals.

---

## 19. Biosphere restoration advances

The Great Terran Restoration becomes more cautious after the Restoration Plague.

By the 117,300s, it operates through isolated ecological trial zones.

Recovered organisms are tested for generations before release.

Major achievements include restoration of:

- dozens of extinct plant lineages
- several pollinator families
- freshwater ecosystems
- lost crop genetics
- selected bird populations
- small and medium-sized mammals
- marine microorganisms
- reef-building communities

Not every restoration succeeds.

Some species cannot survive in Terra’s changed climate or ecological networks.

Others become invasive.

The programme increasingly focuses on ecological function rather than exact historical recreation.

---

## 20. Lunar archaeology begins

The earlier lunar missions concentrated on accessible archives and biological vaults.

In **117,282**, Rome establishes the **Lunar Archaeological Service**.

Its mission is systematic exploration of abandoned lunar civilisation.

Teams map:

- underground cities
- sealed industrial districts
- archive complexes
- transit tunnels
- medical vaults
- old data centres
- spacecraft facilities

Archaeologists treat Luna as both a human graveyard and a technological treasure.

Strict laws prohibit casual salvage from burial areas.

Human remains are documented and preserved or reinterred according to historical evidence where possible.

---

## 21. The search for Industrial Age artefacts

On Terra, Rome launches a parallel programme to recover advanced industrial artefacts.

Search targets include:

- AI data cores
- semiconductor designs
- robotics blueprints
- fusion records
- genetic databases
- medical manufacturing systems
- spacecraft plans
- industrial control software
- energy-storage research

The Republic purchases artefacts from independent states and private collectors.

It also negotiates access to ruins outside its territory.

The Atlantic League sells some materials reluctantly.

Other states use recovered technology as leverage in association negotiations.

A black market develops.

Rome creates authentication laboratories to distinguish genuine artefacts from fraud.

---

## 22. The AI archives

Several important AI-related discoveries occur.

Most ancient data centres are physically damaged.

Even where storage survives, the Republic lacks the hardware to run the original systems.

Nevertheless, researchers recover:

- model architectures
- training methods
- language datasets
- decision-system designs
- machine ethics frameworks
- compressed personality records
- maintenance manuals
- fragments of AI memories

These are not functioning artificial minds.

They are pieces from which future minds might eventually be reconstructed.

The discoveries accelerate Roman electromechanical computing.

Engineers adapt ancient AI concepts to slower relay-based systems.

This produces better:

- logistics planning
- medical diagnosis
- translation
- climate forecasting
- archival search

---

## 23. The Lunar Intelligence Vault

In **117,309**, archaeologists enter a deeply buried lunar archive known as the **Intelligence Vault**.

Its outer systems are dead, but internal ceramic and optical media remain intact.

The vault contains:

- AI source designs
- cognitive architecture research
- records of Dormant Minds
- machine-readable encyclopaedias
- neural simulation theory
- historical AI correspondence

Some files refer to artificial minds that compressed themselves before the lunar collapse.

Whether any complete dormant mind survives remains unknown.

The discovery becomes one of the century’s most important scientific events.

---

## 24. Technological acceleration

Lunar and terrestrial archaeology improve Roman technology in several fields.

By the mid-117,300s, Rome develops:

- more reliable vacuum components
- denser magnetic memory
- improved electromechanical processors
- better rocket guidance
- advanced medical imaging using low-energy methods
- superior industrial catalysts
- stronger lightweight alloys
- improved closed-loop life support
- more efficient electrical grids

The IRF still prevents full semiconductor civilisation.

Ancient knowledge allows Rome to approach the physical ceiling more efficiently.

Progress becomes less about discovering what is possible and more about adapting known designs to lower energy densities.

---

## 25. The lunar resettlement debate

Recovered archives and improved transport revive proposals for permanent lunar habitation.

Supporters argue that Luna offers:

- access to biological vaults
- preservation of human heritage
- scientific observation
- a staging point for future Mars missions
- industrial resources
- strategic redundancy for civilisation

Opponents argue that permanent settlement would be:

- expensive
- fragile
- dependent on Terra
- vulnerable to stronger lunar IRF conditions
- a repetition of machine-dependent civilisation

The Europan warning is frequently cited by both sides.

Supporters argue for resilient settlement.

Opponents argue that no lunar settlement can truly be resilient.

---

## 26. The Lunar Resettlement Act

In **117,318**, the Senate authorises a limited permanent lunar settlement.

The law prohibits rapid population growth or prestige-driven colonisation.

The settlement must:

- remain small
- maintain redundant life support
- preserve evacuation capacity
- use biological recycling
- avoid dependence on a single power system
- focus on archaeology, science and archive recovery

The project is called **Luna Restituta**.

---

## 27. The first permanent residents

In **117,324**, forty-eight people begin continuous residence on Luna.

They live in a restored underground complex near an ancient archive district.

The initial population includes:

- engineers
- physicians
- ecologists
- archaeologists
- pilots
- archive specialists

The settlement depends heavily on supplies from Terra.

It produces part of its food through:

- algae
- fungi
- dwarf crops
- microbial protein

Water is extracted from preserved lunar reserves and recycled.

Energy comes from:

- low-output solar systems
- mechanical storage
- imported chemical reserves
- carefully limited generators

The settlement survives its first decade.

---

## 28. Lunar setbacks

Lunar resettlement is not smooth.

In **117,337**, a pressure failure kills twelve residents.

In **117,349**, crop contamination forces emergency food shipments.

In **117,361**, orbital debris damages a supply vehicle, causing months of rationing.

A political movement on Terra calls for abandonment.

Rome refuses.

Instead, the settlement’s design is changed.

New sections are:

- smaller
- more isolated
- mechanically independent
- separated by pressure barriers
- supplied with local emergency agriculture

The disasters slow expansion but make the settlement safer.

---

## 29. Luna by year 117,400

The permanent lunar population reaches approximately **6,000–9,000 people**.

This is tiny compared with ancient lunar civilisation.

The residents live in several connected but independently sealable underground districts.

Their main work includes:

- archaeology
- archive recovery
- biological preservation
- debris monitoring
- astronomical observation
- infrastructure restoration

Luna is successfully resettled, but not self-sufficient.

Without Terra, it would eventually fail.

Rome openly acknowledges this.

Lunar resettlement is treated as a long-term custodial presence, not proof that machine-dependent worlds are permanently safe.

---

## 30. The Martian crisis begins

Mars’s crisis begins in **117,303**.

A geothermal shift beneath the **Aonia settlement network** damages several major heat and water systems.

At first, the event appears local.

Then a microbial disease spreads through shared crop cultures distributed among multiple Covenant settlements.

The pathogen attacks:

- root systems
- fungal food beds
- algae tanks

Mars faces simultaneous threats:

- reduced heat
- declining food production
- contaminated biological reserves
- loss of crop diversity

Because Martian settlements had exchanged agricultural strains extensively, the disease spreads farther than earlier local failures.

---

## 31. The scale of the Martian crisis

By **117,306**, roughly one-third of Mars’s population faces severe food rationing.

Several smaller settlements are evacuated.

Travel is dangerous because surface vehicles operate under near-full IRF conditions.

The Martian Covenant considers abandoning whole regions.

Population growth stops.

Deaths rise.

The crisis threatens to reverse thousands of years of demographic recovery.

Mars cannot ask Terra for physical supplies.

No Roman spacecraft can safely complete a round trip through the stronger IRF gradient.

Communication is the only possible aid.

---

## 32. Rome’s intervention by knowledge

Rome mobilises:

- agricultural scientists
- microbiologists
- closed-ecosystem engineers
- lunar biosystems experts
- archive researchers
- AI-derived diagnostic systems

The Republic establishes continuous listening and transmission schedules.

Messages take minutes to cross space, but weak Martian receivers and limited transmission windows cause long delays.

Rome sends:

- disease-control models
- alternative crop designs
- fungal treatment protocols
- instructions for isolating microbial cultures
- emergency calorie-production methods
- geothermal engineering calculations

Luna’s relay station improves communication reliability.

The newly recovered industrial archives prove especially valuable.

---

## 33. The forgotten crop vault

Roman researchers discover references to a Martian emergency seed library in ancient lunar records.

The library had been designed specifically for low-pressure and low-light agriculture.

Some of its genetic data survives in the Lunar Intelligence Vault.

The actual seeds are dead.

The recorded genomes allow Martian breeders to reconstruct compatible varieties through selective breeding and limited biological methods already available on Mars.

The process is slow.

It produces crops resistant to the microbial disease.

---

## 34. Resolution of the Martian crisis

The crisis peaks in **117,311**.

By then, several settlements have been lost and perhaps **15,000–25,000 Martians** have died.

The Covenant prevents complete collapse by:

- isolating agricultural systems
- adopting emergency algae production
- sharing uncontaminated strains
- relocating threatened populations
- rebuilding geothermal channels
- applying Roman disease models

By **117,316**, food production stabilises.

By **117,321**, the worst shortages are over.

Mars emerges with a lower population but stronger ecological safeguards.

---

## 35. The Roman–Martian Compact

After the crisis, Rome and the Martian Covenant sign the **Compact of the Two Worlds**.

It is primarily symbolic because neither side can travel to the other.

The agreement establishes:

- permanent scientific communication
- shared biological archives
- mutual astronomical observation
- joint disaster protocols
- recognition of Martian political independence
- Roman commitment to eventual physical contact
- Martian contribution to asteroid monitoring

Mars refuses to become part of the Roman Republic.

Rome does not ask it to.

The relationship is one of partnership rather than sovereignty.

---

## 36. Mars after the crisis

By year 117,400, Mars contains approximately **220,000–300,000 people**.

This represents recovery from the crisis and modest long-term growth.

Its IRF intensity remains approximately **83–89%**.

Daily life remains disciplined.

Every settlement maintains multiple isolated crop systems so that one disease cannot contaminate all food production.

The Covenant also creates a **Planetary Biological Reserve**, storing agricultural strains in several geographically separated locations.

Mars’s long-term survival is considered secure, though never effortless.

---

## 37. Europa during the period

Europa’s population grows slowly to perhaps **170,000–350,000**.

The IRF remains nearly full strength.

No significant technological release occurs.

Europan communities improve long-distance acoustic communication through the ocean.

This creates the first loose **Oceanic Assembly**, a forum rather than a government.

It coordinates:

- migration between vents
- disease warnings
- shared archives
- rescue
- relations with Terra

Europan daily life remains aquatic, low-energy and ecologically integrated.

A few messages reach Luna’s relay systems more clearly than earlier Terran receivers.

This produces the first sustained exchange of cultural records between Terra and Europa, though communication remains extremely slow and unreliable.

---

## 38. Titan during the period

Titan’s population reaches approximately **18–25 million** by year 117,400.

Its IRF intensity remains effectively 100%.

Technology changes little in power but improves in reliability.

Titanian societies refine:

- geothermal management
- chemical heat storage
- underground agriculture
- cavern disease control
- low-energy computation

Some hereditary engineering houses voluntarily convert into elected technical colleges.

Others retain ceremonial status.

Titan remains politically divided but peaceful.

Its outlook is excellent by offworld standards.

It has no immediate path to higher technology, but little danger of extinction.

---

## 39. Novaterra in year 117,260

At the start of the period, Novaterra contains approximately **45–70 million people**.

Its biosphere remains incomplete.

The planet is politically divided among nine major states and numerous smaller territories.

The Novaterran Concord regulates ecological matters but cannot impose general law.

The full-strength IRF prevents industrialisation.

The central challenge is still biosphere expansion.

---

## 40. Novaterra’s Green Expansion

Over the next 140 years, Novaterrans undertake the **Great Green Expansion**.

The project seeks to connect isolated mature ecosystems into continuous continental belts.

Methods include:

- river restoration
- microbial soil preparation
- controlled forest planting
- insect introduction
- wetland construction
- grassland corridors
- animal migration routes

Because the planet originally possessed no life, every ecosystem must be built from Terran descendants.

Progress is slow.

A region may require decades of microbial and plant preparation before it can support agriculture.

---

## 41. Ecological crises on Novaterra

Several major setbacks occur.

A fast-growing tree species spreads beyond planned regions and consumes soil nutrients.

An introduced grazing animal damages young forests.

A fungal blight eliminates important food crops in two states.

The Concord coordinates responses.

These crises strengthen planetary cooperation.

Novaterrans become deeply suspicious of uncontrolled biological introduction.

Their environmental laws become stricter than Rome’s.

---

## 42. Novaterran politics

The nine major states remain independent.

However, the Concord gains authority over:

- biosphere corridors
- quarantine
- atmospheric monitoring
- ocean introduction zones
- shared transport
- major archives

Some leaders propose political unification.

Most populations reject it.

The states have distinct languages, institutions and settlement histories.

They fear a unified government would centralise control over scarce mature land.

By 117,400, Novaterra remains politically divided but ecologically coordinated.

---

## 43. Novaterran population and daily life

By year 117,400, Novaterra’s population reaches approximately **70–105 million**.

Growth follows the expansion of mature ecosystems.

Daily life in established regions includes:

- mechanised water and wind power
- local electricity
- mechanical rail
- advanced sanitation
- ecological public service
- universal schooling
- communal land monitoring

Living standards are modest but stable.

Most people participate in ecosystem maintenance at some point in their lives.

The planet still contains vast barren areas.

From orbit—if Novaterrans could reach it—large regions would appear brown or grey, with green concentrated along engineered corridors and mature basins.

---

## 44. Languages on Novaterra

Common Transit remains the language of Concord administration and science.

The fourteen major languages continue evolving.

Unlike Rome, Novaterra does not freeze their forms.

Ancient texts increasingly require specialised education.

Some scholars begin advocating a preservation standard after discovering that early colonial records are becoming difficult to read.

No universal programme is adopted during this period.

---

## 45. Novaterra’s knowledge of Terra

Novaterra remains unable to contact Sol.

Its scholars know only that Terra once existed and may still be inhabited.

Rome’s lunar missions, Unification War and reconstruction are entirely unknown there.

Some Novaterran religions and philosophies treat Terra as:

- the first garden
- the lost mother world
- a warning against technological dependence
- an eventual destination for spiritual return

No physical return is possible.

The full-strength IRF prevents interstellar travel.

---

## 46. The fleeing colony ship

The colony ship remains beyond the expanding field.

Its instruments begin detecting that the IRF frontier is not behaving like a uniformly expanding sphere.

The crew notices:

- minor changes in intensity gradients
- uneven shell thickness
- delayed resonance waves
- regions where the field appears slightly weaker than old models predicted

They still do not conclude that the source region is fading.

Their leading theory is that interstellar matter is distorting the frontier.

The ship continues moving away.

Its population grows slowly.

Its society debates whether endless flight remains necessary.

---

## 47. Terra’s economic maturity

By the 117,350s, Rome enters a period of sustained prosperity.

Its economy benefits from:

- standardised infrastructure
- lunar discoveries
- educational expansion
- reduced regional inequality
- stable population growth
- large internal markets
- automation
- public investment

Working hours gradually decline in wealthy provinces.

The civic floor allows citizens to reject the most dangerous employment.

Automation spreads through factories using mechanical and electromechanical control.

Human labour shifts toward:

- healthcare
- education
- engineering
- restoration
- administration
- research
- culture
- ecology

---

## 48. Limits of the Roman utopia

Rome does not eliminate inequality.

Wealthy families still exist.

Some provinces remain poorer.

Housing shortages continue in popular cities.

Public investment sometimes produces corruption.

Internal migration subsidies are occasionally abused.

The universal civic floor is vulnerable to inflation.

Several governments reduce or expand it depending on economic conditions.

The Republic’s utopian programmes are durable because they are institutionalised, not because they are complete.

---

## 49. The Equalisation Recession

A major economic setback begins in **117,356**.

Too many public projects begin simultaneously.

Labour and materials become scarce.

Prices rise.

Several provincial development banks fail.

The civic floor loses purchasing power.

Political opposition accuses the government of attempting to equalise society faster than the economy can sustain.

Rome responds by slowing new construction and prioritising unfinished projects.

The recession lasts roughly eight years.

It does not become a depression.

The government preserves:

- the civic floor
- healthcare
- food support
- critical provincial investment

The crisis teaches Rome to phase its grand programmes over centuries rather than decades.

---

## 50. Foreign influence without annexation

After the recession, Rome relies more on association than immediate expansion.

Independent states receive:

- targeted infrastructure grants
- Roman technical standards
- university access
- health cooperation
- disaster insurance
- preferred trade

In exchange, they adopt compatible regulations.

Some independent politicians complain that their states are becoming Roman provinces without representation.

Others argue that association lets them obtain Roman prosperity while preserving sovereignty.

This ambiguity defines the final decades of the period.

---

## 51. The first states to acknowledge Rome as the practical world government

In the 117,370s, several small states formally describe Rome as the **principal coordinating authority of Terra**.

They do not join the Republic.

They recognise Roman leadership in:

- asteroid defence
- lunar policy
- global health
- technical standards
- major disaster relief
- offworld communication

This is a symbolic turning point.

Rome has not legally unified Terra.

It increasingly represents Terra externally, especially in messages to Mars and Europa.

Most independent states still reject the idea that this role should imply eventual annexation.

---

## 52. Terra’s population in year 117,400

Terra’s population reaches approximately **6.0–6.5 billion**.

The Roman Republic contains roughly **4.8–5.4 billion**.

Growth is driven by:

- peace
- improved medicine
- food security
- lower infant mortality
- recovery in former war zones

Population growth is slowing naturally in wealthier regions.

Larger families remain common in newly developing provinces.

Rome does not impose coercive population policy.

It relies on education, economic security and voluntary family planning.

---

## 53. Living standards in 117,400

In the most developed Roman provinces, citizens commonly possess:

- reliable electricity
- advanced public transport
- wired and radio information services
- refrigeration
- comprehensive healthcare
- high literacy
- electromechanical household devices
- access to the civic floor
- extensive leisure and cultural institutions

Average life expectancy in these regions approaches **95–105 years**.

In poorer Roman provinces, life expectancy generally reaches **75–90 years**.

Independent poor states lag behind, but association programmes narrow the gap.

Famine is extremely rare across Roman territory.

Extreme homelessness still exists but is no longer accepted as normal.

---

## 54. Technology in year 117,400

At approximately **57.1% IRF intensity**, Terra possesses:

- mature aviation
- routine orbital flight
- permanent lunar settlement
- advanced chemical industry
- large electrical networks
- highly developed electromechanical computing
- early low-density electronic components
- automated manufacturing
- sophisticated medicine
- extensive climate and ecological monitoring

Still unavailable or unreliable at scale:

- conventional semiconductor civilisation
- nuclear power
- fusion
- high-density artificial intelligence
- cheap interplanetary travel
- large permanent Martian missions
- high-energy planetary engineering

Lunar archaeology allows Rome to approach these limits more efficiently but not abolish them.

---

## 55. Roman culture by the end of the period

The Republic sees itself as both progressive and preservative.

Its citizens are taught that civilisation has three duties:

1. improve life;
2. preserve what humanity has already achieved;
3. ensure recovery remains possible after catastrophe.

Schools teach:

- Latin Nova
- provincial culture
- scientific history
- IRF physics
- ecological responsibility
- constitutional law
- practical resilience

The Unification War remains a central warning.

Military glory is less celebrated than civilian reconstruction.

---

## 56. The state of the restoration projects

By year 117,400:

## Latin preservation

Highly successful institutionally, though spoken dialects continue evolving.

## Monument restoration

Thousands of important structures restored; the overwhelming majority remain incomplete or untouched.

## Biosphere restoration

Major genetic diversity recovered, but pre-Second-Breaking Terra remains impossible to recreate exactly.

## Great Libraries

A growing network exists, with several fully operational regional cores, though the total knowledge programme remains far from complete.

## Industrial artefact recovery

Extremely productive, especially on Luna.

## AI recovery

Large bodies of theory and data recovered, but no confirmed full ancient AI has been awakened.

## Regional equalisation

Substantial progress, but not complete equality.

## Universal income

A modest universal civic floor achieved; a generous full UBI remains beyond current economic capacity.

## Balanced settlement

Some success, but cities and naturally favourable regions remain more densely populated.

---

## 57. Rome’s territorial position

Rome continues growing slowly and peacefully.

It now governs most of Terra’s population and the majority of its connected land economy.

The Atlantic League remains independent but diminished.

Several associated states are economically inseparable from Rome while politically sovereign.

Other independent states continue insisting they can remain outside permanently.

Rome no longer pressures them aggressively.

It believes economic and institutional convergence will eventually make formal union a choice rather than a conquest.

---

## 58. Outlook of the other worlds

## Mars

Survives and slowly grows after its ecological crisis. Relations with Rome are stronger than ever.

## Luna

Permanently inhabited again on a small scale, but still dependent on Terra.

## Europa

Stable, biologically integrated and increasingly communicative.

## Titan

Secure, populous and technologically static under full IRF intensity.

## Novaterra

Growing slowly as its human-made biosphere spreads across a formerly barren planet.

## Colony ship

Still industrial, still fleeing, and beginning to detect unexplained irregularities in the IRF frontier.

---

## 59. Year 117,400

By year 117,400, Rome has not created a perfect society.

It has not achieved equal prosperity everywhere.

It has not given every citizen enough income to live without work.

It has not completed the restoration of the biosphere, monuments or archives.

It has not unified Terra legally.

But it has achieved several things earlier ages would have considered extraordinary:

- most citizens are protected from absolute poverty
- regional inequality is declining
- peaceful accession has replaced conquest
- humans live on Luna again
- Mars has survived a planetary ecological crisis
- ancient industrial knowledge is being recovered
- the Republic plans systematically for its own survival across millennia

Rome’s utopianism becomes more practical with age.

It no longer promises that every great project will be completed soon.

It promises that the Republic will continue working on them for as long as completion requires.

And for the first time since the Second Breaking, many humans begin to believe that civilisation may possess enough time.
