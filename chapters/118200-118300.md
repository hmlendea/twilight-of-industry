# 118,200–118,203 — The demographic shock becomes visible

Roman statisticians had predicted a population decline.

They underestimated its speed.

Across Terra, the largest population cohort consists of citizens aged roughly ninety to one hundred twenty. Many are retired, medically dependent, or approaching the end of even Rome’s extended life expectancy.

Deaths begin rising sharply from:
- ordinary age-related decline;
- delayed effects of the Second Wave;
- chronic illnesses whose treatment was interrupted;
- frailty aggravated by emergency relocation;
- long-term stress.

At the same time, the number of working-age adults is unusually small.

The generation that should have entered the workforce during these years was never born in normal numbers.

Roman society contains:
- enormous numbers of elderly citizens;
- comparatively few middle-aged workers;
- a rapidly growing child population;
- fewer young adults than either group requires.

---

## 118,201 — The Great Age Audit

The World of Roman Terra conducts a complete age-distribution census.

Its conclusions are severe.

In some provinces:
- more than 31% of the population is above ordinary retirement age;
- fewer than 12% are between twenty and forty;
- schools are expanding while workplaces close;
- hospitals and care institutions consume more labour than manufacturing.

The Republic faces a temporary but acute dependency crisis.

Every working-age adult supports, directly or through taxation:
- elderly relatives;
- public pensions;
- medical systems;
- a rapidly growing number of children.

Automation helps, but Roman regulations still limit systems lacking adequate resilient backups.

---

## 118,202 — The delayed birth wave

Fear of the burst had suppressed births.

Its end releases decades of postponed family formation.

Births rise extremely rapidly after 118,170 and peak during the early 118,200s.

Citizens who had delayed parenthood now have children at the same time as younger generations entering family life normally.

Roman hospitals report:
- record maternity demand;
- shortages of paediatric specialists;
- crowded nurseries;
- rapid school construction;
- severe childcare labour shortages.

The demographic structure becomes hourglass-shaped:
- many elderly;
- few working-age adults;
- many children.

This is healthier for the distant future than continued low fertility, but extremely difficult in the present.

---

# 118,203 — The Second Wave Audit begins

Rome establishes the **Commission for the Complete Review of the Second Resonance Wave**.

Unlike earlier emergency reports, this inquiry covers the entire period from:
- the first stockpile census in 118,100;
- pre-burst education and preparation;
- the wave itself;
- sixty years of elevated IRF;
- the return to baseline;
- post-emergency migration and reconstruction.

The Commission has authority to inspect:
- central ministries;
- world governments;
- provincial administrations;
- military logistics;
- private contractors;
- hospital systems;
- Wound research centres;
- Lunar transport authorities.

Its hearings are public unless they involve dangerous IRF research or personal medical data.

---

## 118,204–118,211 — What the audit finds

The audit identifies major successes.

### Successful policies

- Class100 food, water and pressure systems worked.
- School-based civil-protection training saved millions.
- Neighbourhood first-aid networks reduced hospital overload.
- Martian-style gradual shutdown drills would have helped Terra.
- Lunar monoculture regulations prevented famine.
- Distributed libraries preserved technical instructions when networks failed.
- The Roman Worlds structure allowed local adaptation after initial confusion.
- Slow, low-technology Terra–Luna transport remained reliable.

It also identifies serious failures.

### Major failures

- Overcrowded cities had insufficient distribution capacity.
- Some stockpiles existed only statistically and were stored too far from users.
- Conditional medical systems were used without enough trained fallback staff.
- Procurement corruption reduced the quality of emergency goods.
- Foreign associated states had been allowed to believe Roman aid would be greater than legally possible.
- Central emergency administrators sometimes ignored provincial knowledge.
- Several evacuation plans assumed functioning electric lifts and transport.
- Demographic effects had been treated as secondary.
- Officials communicated uncertainty poorly, especially regarding wave duration.

---

## 118,206 — Corruption prosecutions

The audit uncovers extensive corruption.

Crimes include:
- falsifying food-reserve totals;
- substituting low-quality medicine;
- selling emergency fuel privately;
- diverting Lunar transport slots;
- accepting bribes for relocation priority;
- constructing nominal shelters that could not operate without conditional power.

Several hundred senior officials and contractors are prosecuted.

Dozens receive life imprisonment.

Assets are confiscated and transferred to:
- survivor compensation;
- provincial emergency reserves;
- public hospitals.

The prosecutions restore some public trust, but also reveal how close several regions came to preventable collapse.

---

## 118,208 — The Revised Continuity Code

Rome rewrites its disaster laws.

New rules require:
- stockpiles to be geographically distributed;
- emergency inventories to be physically inspected;
- manual access to high-rise buildings;
- reserve medical staff trained in low-equipment practice;
- local officials to retain a formal voice under central emergency control;
- public communication to include ranges of uncertainty;
- associated states to receive explicit aid-capacity estimates;
- demographic recovery to be treated as disaster policy.

Every province must conduct an independent readiness test at least once every eight years.

Unannounced partial drills become normal throughout Rome, though less severe than Martian exercises.

---

# 118,205–118,220 — Rome struggles with fertility

The post-wave birth surge does not automatically create a stable fertility rate.

It is heavily concentrated among families who had postponed children. Once this backlog is exhausted, births begin falling again.

Young citizens cite:
- high housing costs;
- demanding careers;
- long education;
- difficulty caring for both children and elderly parents;
- uncertainty about future resonance waves;
- preference for smaller families.

Rome avoids compulsory reproduction.

It expands:
- free childcare;
- family housing;
- parental civic-income supplements;
- fertility treatment;
- flexible working hours;
- eldercare services;
- school meal programmes.

By **118,218**, fertility across Roman Terra stabilises near replacement level, but not clearly above it.

Some poorer and newly admitted provinces remain above replacement.

The wealthiest provinces remain below.

Rome succeeds in preventing another demographic collapse, but fails to produce a lasting population boom comparable to Mars.

---

# 118,207 — The productivity crisis

Labour shortages become acute.

The most affected sectors are:
- eldercare;
- medicine;
- education;
- construction;
- food distribution;
- infrastructure maintenance;
- Class100 manufacturing.

Some provinces raise retirement ages.

This helps only briefly because many elderly citizens are no longer healthy enough to work full-time.

The civic floor remains, but the pension system is restructured so that retirement becomes more flexible rather than tied to one age.

Citizens may move between:
- full employment;
- reduced work;
- public service;
- partial retirement.

---

## 118,209 — Class75 fallbacks become legal

Rome revises its redundancy rules.

Previously, critical conditional systems normally required Class100 or Class100-Compatible backup.

The new law permits **Class75 fallback systems** where:
- no Class100 alternative is technically available;
- the Class75 system is mechanically isolated;
- failure would degrade capacity rather than cause immediate catastrophe;
- emergency reserves can bridge the remaining gap;
- plans exist to restore a Class100 fallback when one becomes possible.

Class100 remains mandatory for the irreducible survival core of:
- water;
- air on Luna;
- basic food;
- emergency medicine;
- shelter;
- communications;
- pressure safety.

The change allows much greater automation in areas previously limited by the absence of Class100 equivalents.

---

## 118,211–118,230 — The Automation Expansion

Roman industry introduces:
- Class50 production machinery;
- Class75 fallback control;
- Class100 emergency shutdown;
- human oversight.

Automation grows in:
- warehouses;
- rail sorting;
- agriculture;
- eldercare assistance;
- hospital logistics;
- construction;
- waste processing;
- archive indexing.

The productivity crisis eases.

New failures appear.

Some provinces become dependent on imported Class75 parts.

Rome therefore requires large strategic reserves and archived manufacturing designs.

Automation does not eliminate labour scarcity, but it allows the smaller working population to support both elderly citizens and children.

---

# 118,212 — Accession moves to the front of the queue

The demographic decline changes Roman policy toward the remaining independent states.

Before the crisis, accession had been delayed because Rome lacked administrative capacity.

Now Rome needs:
- working-age citizens;
- doctors;
- engineers;
- agricultural labour;
- new provincial economies.

The central government establishes the **Priority Accession Office**.

Applications are ranked by:
- democratic consent;
- administrative stability;
- age structure;
- infrastructure compatibility;
- humanitarian need;
- ability to integrate without immediate military occupation.

Rome still refuses forced annexation.

It now processes willing applicants rapidly.

---

## 118,214 — Immigration laws are liberalised

Citizens of independent Terran states receive expanded rights to:
- work in Rome;
- study in Roman universities;
- settle in underpopulated provinces;
- acquire Roman citizenship;
- bring close relatives;
- access public healthcare after a qualifying period.

The policy is controversial.

Some Romans argue that foreign labour is being used instead of improving domestic working conditions.

Others note that the remaining independent states are already dependent on Roman systems and likely to join eventually.

Immigration is concentrated in provinces with labour shortages rather than overcrowded metropolitan centres.

---

# 118,216–118,228 — The first accessions after the Great Refusal

Three independent states join Rome.

Their accession is eased by the earlier humanitarian dependency.

Each becomes several provinces within Roman Terra.

The transitions include:
- assumption of public debt;
- extension of the civic floor;
- universal healthcare;
- replacement of failing infrastructure;
- Latin administrative education;
- preservation of one secondary official language per province.

One accession is exceptionally smooth.

Another produces protests from citizens who fear loss of sovereignty but not enough support to overturn the referendum.

The third exposes enormous hidden debts and corruption, requiring years of Roman emergency administration.

---

# 118,218 — The Permanent Settlement Resilience Act

The Emergency City Limits Act is made permanent and expanded.

A **global maximum population-density threshold** is established for Roman settlements.

No city may receive a legal threshold above it.

Each settlement is then assigned a lower bespoke threshold based on:
- water capacity;
- emergency food storage;
- hospital resilience;
- evacuation routes;
- low-energy transport;
- local power;
- waste processing;
- shelter capacity;
- fire control;
- geographic risk.

A well-built city may approach the global maximum.

A poorly supplied city must remain far below it.

---

## 118,219 — How the density rules are enforced

Rome avoids direct population removal except during immediate danger.

Instead, it uses:
- construction permits;
- infrastructure-capacity reviews;
- variable land taxes;
- incentives for businesses to relocate;
- housing grants in underpopulated regions;
- limits on new high-density residential districts;
- mandatory infrastructure expansion before further building;
- regional public-employment programmes.

Existing residents are not expelled merely because a city exceeds its threshold.

Over-threshold settlements enter a **deconcentration plan**.

The plan must reduce density over decades through:
- natural population change;
- housing construction elsewhere;
- voluntary relocation;
- decentralised workplaces;
- satellite municipalities.

Emergency exceptions exist for refugee intake, but require compensating infrastructure.

The Constitutional Court rules that thresholds must be transparent, evidence-based and appealable.

---

# 118,221 — The Latin Shift Report

Roman sociologists publish the first comprehensive study of post-wave migration and language.

It confirms that Latin has become the primary home language in far more regions than predicted.

Causes include:
- relocation from Latin-dominant megacities;
- mixed families;
- national education;
- public administration;
- movement between provinces;
- Lunar migration;
- decline of isolated rural populations.

In many newly expanded settlements, migrants from several linguistic backgrounds use Latin with one another. Their children inherit it as their first language.

---

## 118,222 — Provincial linguistic change

By this date:
- Latin-first speakers form a majority in most provincial capitals on Terra;
- several entire provinces now have Latin as the majority native language;
- many secondary official languages remain widely spoken but increasingly as heritage or regional languages;
- Luna is more linguistically uniform than Terra.

The World of Roman Terra expands funding for:
- local-language schools;
- regional media;
- literature;
- archival preservation;
- bilingual public service.

The policy slows language loss but does not reverse the social advantage of Latin.

Rome’s preservation programme succeeds beyond its original purpose.

Latin does not merely remain stable.

It becomes the everyday language of a growing majority of Romans.

---

# 118,224 — The Lunar impact

A small asteroid, approximately nine metres across, approaches Luna from a poorly monitored direction.

Its path passes through observational gaps created by:
- debris clutter;
- reduced post-wave sensor resolution;
- overreliance on Terra-centred tracking;
- assumptions that an object so small could not cause strategic damage.

The asteroid strikes the outer section of the **Civitas Selene Medical-Residential Complex**.

It penetrates:
- surface shielding;
- two empty service corridors;
- a pressurised residential chamber.

Emergency doors seal automatically.

More than 1,900 people die from:
- decompression;
- fragmentation;
- structural collapse.

The complex survives.

The fact that such a small object reached an inhabited district without warning shocks Luna.

---

## 118,224–118,226 — The investigation

Investigators find that no single institution committed gross negligence.

Instead, the failure resulted from overlapping assumptions.

- Terra tracked larger planetary threats.
- Luna tracked debris and known local objects.
- Provincial observatories expected the world government to monitor natural impactors.
- The world government expected central planetary defence to do so.
- Small-object detection had lower funding priority.

The Roman Worlds structure had created a jurisdictional gap.

The families of victims call it “the space between authorities.”

---

## 118,227 — The Lunar Shield Programme

The asteroid-defence programme is expanded around Luna.

New systems include:
- wide-field optical telescopes;
- deep-radar arrays;
- surface impact sensors;
- high-orbit observation platforms;
- small-object interceptor craft;
- better integration with Martian observations;
- mandatory data sharing between central and Lunar authorities.

Habitat regulations also change.

New Lunar districts require:
- thicker fragmented shielding;
- wider empty buffer chambers;
- improved external compartmentalisation;
- shelter zones below residential levels.

The programme is expensive but politically unquestioned.

---

## 118,229 — The hidden stream hypothesis

Analysis of the impactor’s composition and trajectory suggests it may not have been isolated.

It shares unusual mineral characteristics with several tiny objects detected afterward.

Astronomers propose that it belongs to a dispersed ancient debris stream crossing the Terra–Luna system only occasionally.

The stream’s source is unknown.

Possibilities include:
- a shattered asteroid;
- debris from an ancient collision;
- material disturbed by the original impact events;
- remnants of forgotten Industrial Age mining.

The hypothesis remains unconfirmed during this century.

Its later investigation will uncover a much larger danger.

---

# 118,225 — Mars proposes Class65

The Martian Confederacy passes its own conditional-technology law.

Mars defines **Class65** systems as equipment reliable at or above 65% IRF intensity with a safety margin.

The Confederacy will authorise broad deployment only when every permanently inhabited Martian settlement has stabilised below the Class65 threshold.

Current Martian intensity ranges from roughly 65% in favourable regions to above 70% in others.

Deployment therefore remains limited to experiments.

Class100 remains mandatory for:
- air;
- water;
- food;
- pressure control;
- emergency communications;
- shelter;
- essential medicine.

---

## 118,226 — Mars founds its space programme

The Confederacy creates the **Martian Astronautical Directorate**.

Its initial goals are modest:
- high-altitude atmospheric rockets;
- orbital tracking;
- communication satellites;
- study of Phobos and Deimos;
- eventual uncrewed orbit;
- much later, crewed flight.

Mars’s challenges differ from Terra’s historical space race.

Advantages include:
- lower gravity;
- thin atmosphere;
- knowledge from Rome;
- access to ancient Martian facilities.

Disadvantages include:
- much stronger IRF;
- tiny industrial capacity;
- limited propellant production;
- fragile life-support economy;
- dispersed settlements.

A failed launch on Mars can consume resources equivalent to a settlement’s annual infrastructure budget.

---

## 118,227 — The Roman–Martian Astronautical Exchange

Mars asks Rome for:
- early Roman rocket designs;
- space-race failure records;
- low-tech guidance methods;
- orbital mathematics;
- historical sabotage and safety studies;
- debris-tracking systems;
- crew-survival procedures.

Rome agrees.

In return, Mars offers something not previously shared in full:

### The Ares Atmospheric and Biospheric Model

This is the Confederacy’s complete integrated model of:
- low-pressure atmospheric circulation;
- dust electrostatics;
- closed-ecosystem failure;
- microbial soil formation;
- radiation-driven biological mutation;
- planetary water loss.

It is the most advanced low-pressure planetary ecology model in the Solar System.

Rome receives full research access.

The model significantly improves:
- Terra Restituta;
- Lunar agricultural planning;
- Haven signal interpretations once contact ever occurs;
- Roman designs for future colonies.

Mars also grants Roman scientific missions guaranteed future access to Phobos and Deimos under Martian sovereignty.

---

## 118,229–118,238 — Martian rocket experiments

The first Martian test rockets are small and unreliable.

They use:
- low-pressure chemical engines;
- mechanically timed ignition;
- optical tracking;
- Class100-compatible control;
- simple solid propellants.

Milestones include:
- 118,230: first stable ground firing;
- 118,232: first rocket reaches 18 kilometres;
- 118,235: first vehicle crosses 100 kilometres but loses telemetry;
- 118,237: first recovery capsule returns intact.

Failures are frequent.

One launch-pad explosion kills 143 people.

Mars creates extremely wide safety zones around test sites.

Progress is faster than early Roman rocketry because Mars has detailed historical guidance, but it is constrained by a stronger field and a much smaller economy.

---

# 118,231 — The elderly mortality peak

Terran deaths reach their highest annual level.

The retirement-age population contracts rapidly.

Many families lose multiple elderly relatives within a few years.

The social effects are profound.

- Inheritances transfer property to younger generations.
- Large homes become vacant.
- Pension expenditures fall.
- Hospitals gradually free capacity.
- Ancient personal memories of the pre-burst decades disappear.
- Volunteer organisations lose experienced members.

The state enters a painful transition.

The fiscal burden of retirement begins to decline, but the cultural and familial loss is immense.

---

## 118,234 — The younger society emerges

By the middle of the century’s first third, Terra’s age profile changes sharply.

The population is smaller, but the percentage of:
- children;
- teenagers;
- young parents

is much higher.

School systems become the largest branch of many provincial governments.

The state redirects funds from pensions and chronic eldercare toward:
- education;
- housing;
- childcare;
- family health;
- vocational training.

Rome becomes demographically younger even before it stops shrinking.

---

# 118,236 — The Fleeing Ship departs Harbour

The original Fleeing Ship completes repair, resupply and structural inspection.

Its inhabited population has fallen dramatically because settlers remain on Haven and nearby habitats.

The ship resumes its original outward journey with approximately **620,000–780,000 people**.

Its formal maximum permitted population is reduced to **1.1 million**, far below its restored theoretical capacity.

The revised rules require:
- large unused safety margins;
- permanent replacement-part reserves;
- no dismantling of unique manufacturing equipment;
- independent review before habitat expansion;
- automatic reproductive limits near warning thresholds;
- mandatory long-term maintenance accounting;
- multiple redundant bearing systems.

The ship is physically close to its restored original condition.

Its culture is permanently changed by the overcrowding crisis.

---

## 118,238 — The Continuation Charter

Before departure, the ship adopts a revised constitution.

No generation may treat the vessel’s emergency capacity as normal living capacity.

Unique industrial systems may not be dismantled without manufacturing replacements first.

Population growth must be tied to:
- food margin;
- air margin;
- water margin;
- maintenance labour;
- replacement-part inventory.

The original ship continues into interstellar space.

For the first time, it leaves behind a large human population rather than an abandoned system.

---

# 118,240 — Haven’s Settler Commonwealth

The permanent settlers create the **Haven Commonwealth**.

It governs:
- the primary settlement planet;
- local moons;
- asteroid industries;
- terraformation zones.

The Commonwealth remains technologically advanced and heavily automated.

Its greatest project is ecological creation.

Population growth is controlled but much less restricted than aboard the ship because land and industrial capacity are expanding.

---

## 118,243 — The Survey Fleet launches

The first two smaller ships built in Harbour depart toward different nearby systems.

Their missions are:
- identify additional habitable planets;
- establish independent branches of humanity;
- avoid repeating dependence on one world.

Each carries tens of thousands of people.

Their political cultures differ.

One is scientifically centralised.

The other is organised as a federation of habitat communities.

Neither heads toward Sol.

---

## 118,247 — The Return Fleet begins construction

The faction seeking the IRF shell begins building three specialised return vessels.

Their purpose is not yet to reach Sol.

They will:
- approach the shell;
- map intensity;
- test entry;
- investigate whether inner regions are habitable;
- search for evidence of the source.

Because the vessels may enter strong IRF, their essential systems are designed for progressive technological descent.

They will use unrestricted technology initially, then shift toward low-energy modes.

Launch is expected early in the next century.

---

# 118,242 — Roman population begins contracting rapidly

The child population remains large, but it cannot yet replace the elderly dying during this period.

Terra’s total population begins falling by hundreds of millions annually at the peak.

Abandoned housing appears even while some cities remain overcrowded.

This apparent contradiction occurs because:
- population decline is uneven;
- popular cities remain dense;
- peripheral regions lose older populations quickly;
- children need family housing but not workplaces;
- empty homes may be located far from jobs and services.

The Equalisation Programme redirects migrants and young families into declining regions.

Some towns revive.

Others are consolidated into larger regional centres.

---

## 118,245 — The Managed Contraction Doctrine

Rome formally accepts that population decline cannot be reversed immediately.

Its objective becomes preventing disorderly collapse.

Policies include:
- merging underused public institutions;
- preserving rail links to shrinking communities;
- converting abandoned housing;
- relocating services without abandoning residents;
- maintaining ecological restoration on depopulated land;
- encouraging immigration;
- stabilising fertility near replacement.

The state refuses to treat every declining settlement as failure.

Some land is deliberately returned to:
- agriculture;
- forest;
- floodplain;
- wildlife corridors.

Demographic contraction begins supporting Terra Restituta.

---

# 118,248 — The Second Wave Final Report

After forty-five years of work, Rome publishes the complete audit.

The report contains:
- millions of pages;
- regional case studies;
- casualty estimates;
- infrastructure diagrams;
- corruption records;
- demographic models;
- recommendations for future resonance waves.

A shorter version becomes compulsory in schools and public administration.

Its central conclusion is:

> Redundancy prevented collapse, but concentration converted degradation into catastrophe.

The report becomes one of the defining documents of the Dark Age.

Every Great Library receives copies in:
- print;
- engraved summary;
- optical storage;
- low-energy machine-readable form.

# 118,252 — Mars reaches the Class65 threshold locally

Several Martian settlements in favourable IRF regions stabilise below 65%.

The Confederacy authorises limited Class65 use in:
- scientific computation;
- rocket testing;
- atmospheric modelling;
- noncritical industrial control.

Planet-wide deployment remains forbidden until every settlement meets the threshold.

This is politically contentious.

Favourable settlements argue they should not be held back by harsher regions.

The Confederacy maintains the rule to avoid technological inequality dividing Mars.

A compromise allows controlled regional laboratories whose results must be shared nationally.

---

## 118,255 — First Martian orbital attempt

The Astronautical Directorate attempts to place a tiny uncrewed beacon into orbit.

The first vehicle fails during ascent.

The second reaches near-orbital velocity but re-enters.

The third, launched in **118,257**, achieves a highly elliptical orbit for eleven hours before communications fail.

Whether it completes a full orbit becomes a subject of debate.

Rome recognises it as the first probable Martian orbital object of the Confederacy era.

Mars celebrates cautiously.

---

## 118,260 — First confirmed Martian satellite

A simple beacon named **Ares Unitas** reaches stable orbit.

It transmits only:
- timing pulses;
- atmospheric data;
- orbital position;
- a short Latin identification code.

It survives for twenty-three days.

The achievement is comparable to early Roman orbital milestones, but accomplished with fewer launches because Mars possessed historical designs.

It remains extraordinarily expensive for the Martian economy.

The Confederacy promises that space spending will not reduce terraformation funding below constitutional limits.

---

# 118,258 — Rome’s fourth post-wave accession

Another independent state joins the Republic.

Only a small number of sovereign Terran states remain.

Immigration from those states accelerates.

Some independent governments complain that Rome is draining their young workforce before processing accession.

Rome responds with development aid and temporary worker-return programmes.

The demographic gravity of Rome becomes almost impossible to resist.

---

## 118,262 — The Latin-majority generation

Roman schools report that a majority of children across Terra now speak Latin as their first or co-first language.

This does not mean regional languages vanish.

Many citizens are bilingual.

But Latin increasingly dominates:
- urban family life;
- mixed settlements;
- provincial capitals;
- interprovincial marriages;
- Luna.

The distinction between official Latin and everyday Latin narrows because most citizens use the standard regularly.

Regional accents remain strong.

The preservation programme succeeds in preventing fragmentation into separate Latin-derived languages.

---

# 118,265 — The Lunar debris stream is confirmed

New Lunar tracking arrays identify hundreds of small objects sharing orbital characteristics with the 118,224 impactor.

Most are harmless.

Several pass dangerously close to inhabited regions.

The objects form a diffuse stream with a very long period.

Models suggest that denser portions may cross the Terra–Luna system in the future.

The next significant encounter window is predicted for roughly **118,330–118,370**, though uncertainty is high.

Rome and Luna begin planning interceptor reserves.

This danger will become a major issue in the next century.

---

## 118,267 — The buried Industrial origin

Spectroscopy suggests the stream contains both natural rock and refined metal.

The discovery raises the possibility that it includes debris from an Industrial Age asteroid-processing complex.

If so, some fragments may contain:
- machinery;
- fuel;
- sealed industrial chemicals;
- archive modules;
- hazardous materials.

Lunar archaeologists search old records for a matching mining operation.

They find references to a lost mobile refinery, but no confirmed location.

The impact threat and archaeological opportunity become inseparable.

# 118,270 — The elderly burden falls sharply

The high-mortality cohort has largely passed.

Roman pension expenditure falls dramatically.

Healthcare pressure shifts from chronic geriatric care toward:
- paediatrics;
- maternal health;
- injury prevention;
- education-related medicine.

The working-age population remains small, but children begin reaching adolescence.

Rome’s fiscal position improves.

The social mood remains sombre.

An entire generation of grandparents has disappeared within a few decades.

## 118,273 — Fertility settles

Roman fertility stabilises.

Across the Republic as a whole it reaches approximately replacement level, slightly above in some years and slightly below in others.

Rome does not achieve a sustained high-growth demographic regime.

It does achieve stability.

Population will continue falling for some time because the missing generations cannot be replaced immediately.

The government shifts from encouraging maximum births to supporting desired family size without economic penalty.

# 118,276 — The Youth Service Programme

To address labour shortages and integrate the enormous young generation, Rome creates a voluntary public-service system for citizens aged eighteen to twenty-four.

Service options include:
- disaster relief;
- elder support;
- ecological restoration;
- Lunar construction;
- provincial education;
- infrastructure maintenance;
- Great Library work;
- medical assistance.

Participants receive:
- education credits;
- housing priority;
- employment certification;
- civic-income supplements.

The programme is popular and helps bridge the demographic gap.

Critics fear it could evolve into civilian conscription.

Rome keeps participation voluntary.

# 118,280 — Roman Class75 automation matures

Automation reaches a scale capable of offsetting much of the productivity loss.

Roman systems now commonly use three layers:

1. Class50 operating layer for normal high-efficiency work;
2. Class75 fallback layer for degraded conditions;
3. Class100 survival layer for essential minimum service.

This architecture becomes standard in:
- hospitals;
- transit;
- manufacturing;
- logistics;
- agriculture;
- public administration.

It is expensive.

It also proves more flexible than the previous all-or-nothing requirement.

# 118,282 — Mars’s first Phobos mission proposal

The Martian Astronautical Directorate proposes an uncrewed mission to Phobos.

Objectives include:
- mapping;

- testing Martian sovereignty in practice;

Rome offers:

Mars provides Rome with expanded access to the Ares Model and agrees to place a shared Solar Relay Assembly beacon on Phobos.

The mission is approved, but launch is scheduled for the following century.

# 118,284 — Haven’s probe arrival calculations are revised

SSLI revises the Solar-probe schedule using the probes’ actual performance.

The probes accelerate to approximately **0.5c** in IRF-free space.

At that speed, the 2,180-light-year crossing requires roughly **4,360 years** of cruise time as measured in the Solar/Harbour reference frame, ignoring relativistic corrections of modest size at 0.5c.

They do not slow before entering the IRF.

Their engines remain off during high-speed transit through the field.

Long before reaching their targets, they begin low-thrust braking using IRF-compatible systems.

Estimated braking periods range from approximately **140 to 320 years**, depending on destination and surviving propulsion.

Expected arrival windows are therefore roughly:
- Terra probes: 122,720–122,900
- Mars probes: 122,720–122,920
- Jupiter probes: 122,740–122,950
- Saturn probes: 122,750–122,980

These estimates assume:
- no collision;
- no navigation failure;
- successful field entry;
- functioning backup propulsion;
- no unexpected IRF interaction.

The transmitted SSLI signals still arrive far earlier, around **120,333**, because they travel at light speed.

# 118,286 — Novaterra’s post-Concord fragmentation

The weakened Novaterran Concord loses more authority.

Several states establish regional ecological alliances instead.

The planet’s governance becomes layered:
- national governments;
- river-basin councils;
- biosphere corridor treaties;
- quarantine leagues;
- remnants of the Concord.

This decentralisation is messy but not entirely destructive.

Regional institutions sometimes respond better than the planetary Concord did.

However, no authority can impose common rules globally.

As green land expands, disputes increase over:
- migration;
- forest ownership;
- river diversion;
- introduction of species;
- atmospheric projects.

# 118,291 — Europa’s first demographic planning crisis

Blue Vent and several older regions experience rapid population growth.

Europa lacks the energy and transport capacity to relocate people easily.

The Oceanic Assembly adopts voluntary settlement quotas tied to:
- vent output;
- food-web stability;
- air production;
- evacuation capacity.

Some communities reject the quotas as central overreach.

Unlike Mars, Europa still resists full planetary government.

The dispute remains unresolved at century’s end.

# 118,293 — The Fleeing Ship’s new doctrine

The resumed ship reports that its lower population produces:
- larger living spaces;
- better maintenance;
- greater ecological reserves;
- lower system stress;
- improved education;
- restored confidence.

Birth controls are gradually relaxed but remain tied to capacity.

The ship’s population begins growing slowly again.

The Continuation Charter remains popular.

The crisis becomes a foundational historical warning taught to every child aboard.

# 118,295 — Terra’s population reaches its minimum

Terra’s population bottoms out at approximately **17.5–18.5 billion**.

The decline from 118,200 is around **22–27%**.

This is one of the largest peacetime demographic contractions in recorded Roman history.

The population is now far younger proportionally.

The largest cohorts are:
- children;
- teenagers;
- adults in their thirties.

The number of very old citizens is much smaller.

Births and immigration begin exceeding deaths.

Slow recovery starts.

## 118,297 — The Demographic Stabilisation Declaration

Rome announces that the contraction has ended.

The declaration is cautious.

Population growth is expected to remain slow because:
- fertility is only near replacement;
- urban density limits restrict concentrated expansion;
- immigration depends on the small remaining independent population;
- Luna absorbs a growing share of Roman births and migrants.

Rome’s objective becomes stable age structure, not maximum population.

Mars is multiplying rapidly and has taken its first steps into orbit.

Humanity’s worlds are no longer merely surviving the Dark Age.
