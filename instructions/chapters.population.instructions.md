# Population And Demographic Continuity

## Governing Principle

- Treat population as a constrained consequence of food, energy, ecology, habitat, medicine, migration and mortality, not as a freely scalable measure of progress.
- Derive every estimate from the previous canonical census, elapsed time, natural increase, net migration and exceptional losses.
- Do not select a population merely for dramatic scale.
- Preserve established census anchors unless the user explicitly requests a retcon.
- Distinguish biological population growth from territorial accession, administrative reclassification and improved census coverage.
- Distinguish residents, citizens, governed populations, associated populations and refugees whenever those categories differ.
- Match numerical precision to evidence. Use ranges where censuses are incomplete or contact is indirect.

## Mandatory Capacity Check

Before increasing a population, verify the chapter provides sufficient capacity in:
- calories, agricultural land, soil fertility and fertiliser;
- water, sanitation and waste processing;
- energy under the local IRF intensity;
- housing or sealed habitat volume;
- breathable air, heat and pressure control on hostile worlds;
- medicine, maternal care and child survival;
- transport, storage and food distribution;
- ecological resilience and genetic diversity;
- governance, security and disaster redundancy.

Population does not increase merely because a century passes. If one of these constraints remains fixed, explain which other factor compensates for it.

## Growth-Rate Check

- Calculate the implied mean annual rate whenever a population changes substantially: `rate = (end / start)^(1 / years) - 1`.
- For a mature agrarian or low-energy society, sustained net growth of approximately 0–0.15% annually is ordinary; higher rates require expanding food production, improving medicine or recovery from an earlier demographic deficit.
- Sustained growth of approximately 0.15–0.5% annually requires explicit mechanisation, ecological expansion, high fertility, falling mortality or substantial immigration.
- Growth above 0.5% annually across a century is exceptional and must be supported by quantified migration, commissioned habitats, restored settlements or unusually favourable recovery conditions.
- Growth above 1% annually across a century must not occur without an extraordinary and explicit transfer of population plus matching infrastructure.
- On sealed worlds, available life-support capacity limits population before fertility does. Every major increase must correspond to new pressure volume, food production, water reserves, heat rejection and evacuation capacity.
- After war, famine, epidemic or demographic panic, model age structure and delayed mortality. Do not restore the former trajectory immediately after the immediate crisis concludes.

## World-Specific Constraints

### Terra

- Under full-strength IRF and mature low-energy agriculture, Terra stabilises near 1.5–1.7 billion.
- During the renewed industrial transition, mechanised agriculture, sanitation, medicine and distribution gradually increase capacity.
- Under the approximately 50% IRF conditions and institutions of years 28,000–28,550, Terra supports approximately 8–9 billion, not twenty billion or more.
- Exceeding 9 billion requires a documented increase in sustainable calories, water, energy and ecological resilience beyond the current baseline.

### Luna

- Luna has no natural biosphere. Population is constrained by sealed volume, air, water, food, radiation protection, heat rejection and emergency reserves.
- Treat migration permits as habitat-capacity allocations, not merely political decisions.
- Luna remains a world of hundreds of millions through year 28,550, not billions.

### Mars

- Mars can expand through restored caverns, enclosed valleys, water networks and improving industry, but every increase remains tied to pressure-safe habitat and food capacity.
- The 3.1-billion Three-Billion Horizon is a distant cultural aspiration, not a current projection or policy target.
- Pronatal policy may accelerate growth only after habitat, maternal care and food systems expand.

### Europa

- Europa remains constrained by viable vent ecosystems, pressure-safe settlements, nutrient circulation and geological isolation.
- Population estimates remain uncertain because external observation and communication are incomplete.
- Do not convert an uncertain census range into rapid demographic growth without evidence.

### Titan

- Titan remains under approximately full-strength IRF. Growth depends on geothermal access, cavern volume, water, compartmentalised ecology and evacuation latency.
- From the year 28,500 baseline, the approved conservative multi-century path adds approximately 60–100 million residents; coordinated infrastructure may add approximately 100–160 million.
- Do not project billions under current conditions.

### Novaterra

- Novaterra has a naturally survivable but engineered and genetically narrow biosphere under full-strength IRF.
- Its population overshoots near 1.1 billion before the food-system collapse.
- The collapse creates permanent demographic and ecological loss. Do not imply a rapid return to the former maximum.

### Haven And Unrestricted Industrial Societies

- An absent IRF permits substantially faster industrial and demographic expansion, but sterile terrain, ecological maturation, settlement construction and transport still constrain growth.
- Industrial capability does not make an immature planetary biosphere instantly habitable.

- Post-Second-Breaking Terra: approximately 1.8–2.2 billion in 11,379.

- Renewed industrialisation: approximately 2.5–2.9 billion in 25,610, slightly more than 3 billion in 26,610, and approximately 3.8–4.2 billion in 27,110.

- Recovery: approximately 4.8–5.1 billion in 27,260 and approximately 6.0–6.5 billion in 27,400.

## Cross-Chapter Synchronisation

Whenever a population changes, update every dependent statement in the affected chapter:
- frontmatter summary;
- opening world-state table;
- dated census or demographic event;
- midpoint and end-of-period summaries;
- population-share and percentage-change calculations;
- migration, casualty and refugee totals;
- descriptions such as thousands, millions or billions;
- historical comparisons and carrying-capacity conclusions.

The end census of one chapter must equal the opening census of the next chapter unless the intervening time or uncertainty is explicitly documented.

Search adjacent chapters for superseded figures after every demographic revision. Recalculate percentages from correlated midpoint estimates rather than combining incompatible extremes from independent ranges.

## Catastrophes And Recovery

- Quantify losses from the population immediately preceding the event.
- Separate direct deaths, excess mortality, missing births, migration and census uncertainty.
- Ensure survivor categories sum to the stated total.
- Preserve permanent ecological, genetic, institutional and cultural losses after a major collapse.
- A lower population does not itself restore carrying capacity if soil, crops, pollinators, water systems or governance remain damaged.
- Recovery must proceed from the surviving age structure and productive base, not from the pre-crisis growth rate.
