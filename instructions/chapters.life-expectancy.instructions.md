# Life Expectancy and Longevity Continuity

## Standard Measure

- Use **life expectancy at birth** for every population-wide longevity figure unless another measure is explicitly named.
- Life expectancy at birth is the mean number of years a newborn would live under the period's current age-specific mortality rates.
- Do not use `average lifespan`, `typical lifespan`, `maximum lifespan` or `life expectancy` interchangeably.
- When discussing adults who survive childhood, label the measure as **adult lifespan**, **modal age at death**, or **remaining life expectancy at age [number]**.
- Exceptional longevity does not establish the population average. A society may contain people aged 110–130 while life expectancy at birth remains in the eighties or nineties.
- Age structure and life expectancy are separate measures. A large elderly cohort changes population totals and dependency ratios, but does not by itself establish life expectancy at birth.

## Required Mortality Model

Before assigning or changing life expectancy, evaluate:
- infant and childhood mortality;
- maternal mortality;
- infectious disease and vaccination coverage;
- nutrition and food stability;
- sanitation, clean water and waste disposal;
- trauma care and surgical capability;
- chronic disease, cancer and cardiovascular treatment;
- occupational, military and environmental hazards;
- local gravity, radiation, pressure and atmospheric risks;
- access inequality between regions and social groups;
- disaster redundancy and continuity of treatment.

An improvement in one category does not eliminate mortality from the others.

## Medical Capability Thresholds

- Under full-strength IRF, scientific knowledge, sanitation, vaccination, surgery, midwifery and preventive medicine may support high life expectancy, but advanced imaging, precision pharmaceuticals, artificial organs and regenerative medicine remain restricted.
- Under moderately weakened IRF, antibiotics, reliable sterile facilities, imaging, advanced surgery and conditional medical automation become more available. Deployment and equitable access still require institutions, energy and manufacturing.
- Lower IRF intensity does not automatically increase life expectancy. State which medical or public-health capability became available and how widely it was deployed.
- Life expectancy at birth above approximately 95 years requires explicit, broadly accessible control of major chronic diseases and advanced organ support.
- Life expectancy at birth above 100 years requires reliable regenerative medicine, organ replacement, genetic repair or equivalent longevity technology. Reserve this level primarily for the unrestricted Industrial Age and advanced zero-IRF societies.
- Inherited Industrial Age modifications reduce specific risks such as cancer, metabolic disease or radiation injury. Do not treat them as general anti-ageing unless the text explicitly establishes that function.

## Crisis and Recovery Rules

- War, famine, epidemic, sanitation collapse or interrupted chronic care must reduce period life expectancy in proportion to the affected population.
- Separate direct mortality from delayed mortality, missing births and emigration.
- During a severe crisis, report both the global figure and protected-region figure when their conditions diverge substantially.
- Life expectancy may rebound rapidly after an acute crisis if mortality hazards cease, but damaged cohorts, malnutrition, disability and lost medical capacity continue affecting later decades.
- Do not resume the pre-crisis improvement trend without documenting restored food, sanitation, staffing, medicine and security.
- A declining elderly cohort may reduce population while life expectancy at birth remains high; explain the distinction rather than using life expectancy as a proxy for age structure.

## World-Specific Constraints

### Terra

- Mature low-energy Terra can reach approximately 60–80 years through sanitation, nutrition, vaccination and preventive medicine.
- Renewed industrial medicine can raise prosperous regions into the high eighties or nineties.
- Without broadly available regenerative medicine and advanced organ replacement, do not assign a population-wide average above the high nineties.
- Preserve substantial regional inequality until universal infrastructure and healthcare are explicitly achieved.

### Luna

- Account for low gravity, radiation exposure, enclosed-air outbreaks, food monocultures and complete dependence on pressure systems.
- Medical screening and disciplined habitat management improve outcomes, but they do not erase chronic low-gravity and radiation risks.
- Lunar life expectancy remains below prosperous Terra under current conditions.

### Mars

- Account for low gravity, radiation, pressure accidents, toxic dust, restricted diets, settlement isolation and medical staffing limits.
- Inherited adaptations reduce specific hazards but do not eliminate habitat mortality.
- Industrial expansion can improve care while simultaneously increasing occupational and crowding risks.

### Europa

- Account for full or near-full IRF, geological isolation, pressure hazards, restricted vent ecologies and limited evacuation.
- Established communities may achieve stable public health, but Europa remains below Terra, Luna, Mars and Titan in life expectancy through year 28,550.
- Wide ranges may represent incomplete census access, but uncertainty must not be mistaken for improvement.

### Titan

- Account for full-strength IRF, confinement, thermal dependence and evacuation latency.
- Stable food, housing and communal health systems permit gradual gains despite restricted medical technology.
- Improvements come from prevention, nutrition, midwifery, sanitation and accident reduction, not unexplained advanced medicine.

### Novaterra

- Before the food-system collapse, full-strength IRF and a natural atmosphere permit life expectancy comparable to mature low-energy Terra.
- During the collapse, calculate global life expectancy from famine, childhood mortality, epidemic disease, violence and sanitation failure.
- Protected states may remain decades above the global figure. Label both populations explicitly.
- Recovery remains slow while ecology, crop diversity, medical institutions and transport remain damaged.

### Haven and Other Zero-IRF Societies

- Zero IRF permits advanced imaging, automated surgery, precision pharmaceuticals, artificial organs and genetic medicine.
- Life expectancy above 100 years is credible only when those systems are broadly accessible and resilient.
- Frontier accidents, immature ecology, limited population redundancy and unequal access still prevent automatic restoration of Shield Age longevity.

## Canonical Historical Anchors

- Non-industrial 2026 scenario: approximately 40–60 years at birth, depending on region.
- Year 2200: approximately 55–68 years at birth.
- Year 4000: approximately 68–76 years at birth; adults surviving childhood commonly reach their late seventies or early eighties.
- Year 5000 stable regions: approximately 70–78 years at birth.
- Year 11,000 Industrial Age: approximately 105–125 years at birth; adults with continuous advanced treatment commonly live 120–150 years.
- After the Second Breaking: Terran life expectancy at birth falls from above a century to below fifty.
- Year 20,000 stable Terran regions: approximately 62–75 years at birth; poor or isolated regions may remain below forty-eight.
- Year 23,110 stable Terran federations: approximately 68–80 years at birth; prosperous Titanian caverns reach approximately 68–82.
- Near year 25,610, wealthy Terran regions reach approximately 82–90 years at birth.
- Near year 26,610, the healthiest Martian cities reach approximately 68–80 years at birth.
- Near year 27,110, the best Terran regions reach approximately 84–92 years at birth.
- Year 27,400 prosperous Roman regions: approximately 84–92 years at birth; poorer Roman provinces approximately 68–80.
- Year 27,600 prosperous Roman regions: approximately 86–94 years at birth.

## Cross-Chapter Synchronisation

Whenever a longevity estimate changes, update every dependent statement:
- chapter summaries and world-state tables;
- midpoint and end-of-period status blocks;
- claims concerning medical progress or decline;
- retirement ages and dependency ratios;
- descriptions of elderly cohorts;
- crisis mortality and recovery claims;
- comparisons with the Industrial Age;
- adjacent chapter boundary figures.

Search the complete repository for the superseded range after every revision.

The end figure of one chapter must equal the opening baseline of the next unless elapsed time, regional scope or measurement uncertainty explains the difference.

## Plausibility Review

Before finalising an estimate, verify:
- the metric is named;
- the medical capability exists and is broadly deployed;
- infant, maternal and environmental mortality are represented;
- the figure responds to wars, famines, waves and infrastructure failures;
- improvements follow deployed capability rather than IRF decline alone;
- exceptional individuals are not used as the population average;
- offworld habitat risks remain visible;
- numerical ranges have a stated regional or observational basis.
